UNIT / TOPIC 1

Fundamentals of Engineering Drawing

1.1 Meaning and purpose

Engineering drawing is a standard graphical language used to communicate the shape, size, material-related details and manufacturing information of an engineering component. A good drawing should be clear enough that another person can interpret the component without guessing.

In engineering, drawings are used for design communication, manufacturing, inspection, assembly, maintenance and technical documentation. Unlike an artistic sketch, an engineering drawing follows agreed conventions for lines, dimensions, symbols, scales and projections.

1.2 Drawing instruments and their uses

InstrumentPurposeCare / exam point
Drawing boardProvides a flat working surface for the sheet.Keep the working edge clean and the sheet firmly fixed.
Mini drafter / T-squareDraws horizontal and parallel lines; works with set squares for inclined lines.Check alignment with the board edge.
Set squaresDraws standard 30°, 45°, 60° and 90° lines.Use the correct edge and avoid slipping.
ScaleMeasures lengths and transfers dimensions.Do not use a measuring scale as a substitute for a straightedge if its edge is damaged.
CompassDraws circles and arcs.Keep the needle point steady and use a sharp lead.
DividerTransfers distances and divides lengths.Set both legs carefully to avoid changing the distance.
ProtractorMeasures and sets angles.Place its centre and baseline accurately.
French curveDraws smooth irregular curves through selected points.Join curve segments smoothly; do not force one curve through every point.

1.3 Types of lines and their applications

Line types communicate different information. Line thickness and pattern must remain consistent throughout a drawing.

Line typeTypical appearanceUse
Continuous thick__________Visible outlines and visible edges.
Continuous thin──────────Dimension lines, extension lines, projection lines, leader lines and hatching.
Dashed thin– – – – – –Hidden edges and hidden details.
Chain thin— · — · — · —Centres, axes and lines of symmetry.
Chain thick at ends— · — · —Indication of cutting planes where the drawing convention requires it.
Remember: Visible edges are normally drawn more prominently than dimensions and construction lines. Keep construction lines light so that the final view is easy to read.

1.4 Lettering and sheet layout

Lettering should be uniform, legible and consistent. Use the same letter height, inclination, spacing and stroke thickness for similar text. Avoid decorative handwriting.

A drawing sheet is arranged with a border and a title block. The title block normally records identification details such as drawing title, drawing number, scale, date, drafter/checker and sheet number as required by the institution or drawing standard.

1.5 Dimensioning

Dimensioning is the process of specifying the size and location of features on a drawing. A dimension generally consists of a dimension value, dimension line, extension lines and arrowheads or other approved terminators.

Schematic only: the dimension line is separate from the object outline.

Common dimensioning systems

Aligned systemDimension figures are placed parallel to their dimension lines and are read from the bottom or right-hand side of the sheet.
Unidirectional systemAll dimension figures are written horizontally and read from the bottom of the sheet.

Good dimensioning practice

  • Give each required dimension clearly; avoid unnecessary repetition.
  • Place dimensions outside the view wherever practical.
  • Avoid crossing dimension lines and extension lines.
  • Do not use object outlines as dimension lines.
  • Use a consistent unit and follow the drawing standard specified in class.

1.6 Freehand sketching

Freehand sketching is used to communicate an idea quickly without depending on drawing instruments. The sketch should preserve the main proportions, shape, relative positions and important features. Begin with light construction lines, establish the overall envelope, add major features, then darken the final outline.

How to write a short exam answer: “State the uses of engineering drawing”

  1. It communicates component geometry and dimensions.
  2. It provides manufacturing and assembly information.
  3. It supports inspection and quality control.
  4. It records design information for future reference.
  5. It provides a common technical language between design, production and maintenance teams.
UNIT / TOPIC 2

Conic Sections

A conic section is a curve obtained when a plane cuts a right circular cone. The nature of the curve depends on the inclination of the cutting plane with respect to the cone axis and generator.

CurveGeometrical descriptionUseful property
CirclePlane perpendicular to the cone axis.All points are at a constant distance from the centre.
EllipseOblique plane cuts one nappe and is not parallel to a generator.Sum of distances from two foci is constant.
ParabolaPlane parallel to a generator.Distance from focus equals perpendicular distance from directrix.
HyperbolaPlane cuts both nappes.Absolute difference of distances from two foci is constant.

2.1 Ellipse — important terms

The major axis is the longest diameter; the minor axis is the shortest diameter and is perpendicular to the major axis through the centre. The two fixed points are the foci. The eccentricity of an ellipse is less than one.

e = c/a, where 0 < e < 1

Here, a is the semi-major axis and c is the distance from the centre to either focus.

2.2 Parabola — important terms

A parabola has a focus, directrix, axis, vertex and latus rectum. Its eccentricity is one. Every point on the curve is equidistant from the focus and the directrix.

e = 1

2.3 Hyperbola — important terms

A hyperbola consists of two separate branches. It has two foci, two vertices, a transverse axis and asymptotic directions. Its eccentricity is greater than one.

e > 1
Construction-answer sequence: Draw the reference axis and directrix or axes as required; locate the focus/foci and vertex/centre; mark the specified parameters; construct sufficient points using the chosen method; join the points with a smooth continuous curve; label the curve and important elements.

2.4 Cycloid

A cycloid is the locus of a point on the circumference of a circle rolling without slipping along a straight line. One revolution of the generating circle produces one arch. The generating circle is divided into equal parts and the corresponding rolling positions are used to locate successive points of the curve.

UNIT / TOPIC 3

Orthographic Projection

3.1 Principle

Orthographic projection represents a three-dimensional object by projecting its features onto mutually perpendicular reference planes using parallel projectors perpendicular to each plane. The main views are front view, top view and side view.

Typical three-view arrangement. First-angle and third-angle layouts are not interchangeable.

3.2 First-angle projection

In first-angle projection, the object is imagined between the observer and the projection plane. The views are placed opposite to the viewing direction: the top view is placed below the front view, and the right-side view is placed to the left of the front view.

3.3 Third-angle projection

In third-angle projection, the projection plane is imagined between the observer and the object. The top view is placed above the front view, and the right-side view is placed to the right of the front view.

ViewFirst-angle placementThird-angle placement
Top viewBelow front viewAbove front view
Right-side viewLeft of front viewRight of front view
Frequent exam error: Students often draw the correct shape but place the views using the wrong projection method. Write the projection method first, draw the symbol if required, and check the view positions before adding details.

3.4 Projection of points, straight lines and planes

For a point, its front and top projections are located using projectors perpendicular to the reference line. The position of the point relative to the horizontal plane (HP) and vertical plane (VP) determines the quadrant and the positions of its views.

For a straight line, identify its true length and inclinations to HP and VP. A line parallel to a reference plane appears in true length in the view on that plane. A line inclined to a plane generally appears foreshortened in that view.

For a plane surface, first establish its shape and orientation. Determine whether it is parallel, perpendicular or inclined to HP/VP, then obtain the required projections in a logical sequence. Do not assume that the apparent shape in every view is the true shape.

Reliable construction workflow

  1. Draw the reference line XY and mark the given reference positions.
  2. Identify which dimensions are measured from HP and VP.
  3. Draw the initial or simple-position view using the given data.
  4. Apply the required rotation or change-of-position method.
  5. Project corresponding points carefully between views.
  6. Join points in sequence, show hidden details correctly and label the final views.
UNIT / TOPIC 4

Projection and Section of Solids

4.1 Common engineering solids

SolidBase / generating featureImportant axis
PrismPolygonal base with parallel generators.Line joining centres of the two bases.
PyramidPolygonal base with triangular faces meeting at an apex.Line from apex to centre of base.
CylinderCircular base with generators parallel to the axis.Line joining centres of circular bases.
ConeCircular base with generators meeting at an apex.Line from apex to centre of base.
SphereSurface at constant radius from a centre.Any diameter through the centre.

4.2 Projection of solids — what to identify first

Before drawing, identify the solid, base shape, axis length, base diameter or side, and the orientation of the axis with HP and VP. Then decide which view gives the clearest representation of the given position.

4.3 Section of solids

A section is obtained when an imaginary cutting plane passes through a solid. The section view shows the shape formed at the intersection of the cutting plane and the solid. The apparent section depends on the orientation and position of the cutting plane.

Hatching is used to indicate the cut material. Keep hatch lines thin, evenly spaced and consistent. Do not hatch visible outlines or empty spaces.

Procedure for a sectional-solid problem

  1. Draw the required projections of the complete solid in the given position.
  2. Mark the cutting plane in the view where its inclination is specified.
  3. Locate the points where the cutting plane intersects the edges or generators.
  4. Project those intersection points to the other view.
  5. Join the points in their correct sequence to obtain the section boundary.
  6. Hatch the cut surface and draw the required sectional or remaining-solid view.
UNIT / TOPIC 5

Development of Surfaces

Development is the process of unfolding the surface of a three-dimensional object onto a flat plane without stretching or tearing it. It is used in sheet-metal work, fabrication, ducts, hoppers and packaging.

SolidDevelopment principleShape obtained
PrismUnfold the rectangular lateral faces in sequence.Strip of rectangles with base edges repeated.
PyramidUse true lengths of slant edges and construct face triangles.Connected triangular faces.
CylinderUnroll the curved surface; length equals circumference.Rectangle of length πD and height equal to cylinder height.
ConeUse slant height as radius of a sector.Circular sector with radius equal to slant height.
Cylinder lateral development length = πD
Cone sector angle θ = 360° × r/l

Here, D is cylinder diameter, r is cone base radius and l is cone slant height.

Method of development

The parallel-line method is used for prisms and cylinders. The radial-line method is used for pyramids and cones. For transition pieces and complex surfaces, triangulation may be used when specified.

Exam check: State the method, show the true lengths or generators used, divide the circumference or base as required, transfer the divisions in order, and label the final stretch-out clearly.
UNIT / TOPIC 6

Isometric Projection

Isometric projection is a pictorial representation in which the three principal axes are equally inclined to the plane of projection. The three axes are separated by 120° in the isometric drawing.

Isometric axes and scale

In an isometric view, the two receding horizontal axes are commonly drawn at 30° to the horizontal, while the vertical axis remains vertical. In isometric projection, lengths along the isometric axes are reduced using the isometric scale. In an isometric drawing, true lengths are used along the isometric axes.

Isometric projectionUses isometric lengths, approximately 0.816 times the true lengths.
Isometric drawingUses true lengths along the isometric axes; the object appears slightly larger than its isometric projection.
Isometric length ≈ 0.816 × true length

Drawing a simple isometric object

  1. Draw the three isometric axes from a common origin.
  2. Mark the overall length, width and height using the correct scale.
  3. Construct the enclosing isometric box.
  4. Locate steps, slots, holes and inclined faces from the box dimensions.
  5. Draw circles as isometric ellipses where required.
  6. Darken visible edges and remove construction lines.
Common mistake: A circle on an isometric plane is not drawn as a true circle. It appears as an ellipse. Use the prescribed ellipse construction method.
UNIT / TOPIC 7

Perspective Projection

Perspective projection represents an object as it appears to the eye. Projectors converge at a single station point, so parallel edges may appear to meet at vanishing points. This gives a realistic sense of depth.

TypeVanishing pointsTypical use
One-point perspectiveOne principal vanishing point.Object viewed with one face parallel to the picture plane.
Two-point perspectiveTwo vanishing points.Corner view showing two receding faces.
Three-point perspectiveThree vanishing points.Strong high-angle or low-angle view of a tall object.

Elements to label in a perspective construction

  • Station point (SP)
  • Picture plane (PP)
  • Ground plane / ground line (as used in the construction)
  • Horizon line (HL)
  • Vanishing point or points (VP)
  • Visual rays and picture-plane intersections

Basic construction logic

Establish the picture plane and station point, identify the horizon and vanishing point(s), project visual rays from the station point through the object to the picture plane, and obtain the perspective points. Complete the visible edges by joining the points to the correct vanishing points.

HOW TO PRESENT IN THE EXAM

Answer-writing method for drawing questions

  1. Read the data twice. Underline dimensions, inclinations, reference planes and the required final view.
  2. Write the method. Mention first-angle/third-angle, parallel-line/radial-line, or the construction method asked.
  3. Set up the reference. Draw XY, axes, base line or the required reference geometry before constructing the object.
  4. Show construction clearly. Keep projectors and arcs light; label important points and intermediate views.
  5. Emphasise the final answer. Darken visible outlines, show hidden edges only where needed, hatch sections and write view names.
  6. Check the drawing. Verify dimensions, view alignment, projection method and labels before submission.
Teacher's advice: In a graphical subject, the construction is the answer. A neat final view with correct projection logic is more valuable than a long paragraph describing a drawing that has not been constructed properly.

Frequent errors to avoid

  • Mixing first-angle and third-angle view arrangements.
  • Forgetting to project corresponding points between views.
  • Using apparent length instead of true length where a construction requires true length.
  • Hatching the wrong region or drawing hatch lines too dark.
  • Using true circles instead of isometric ellipses.
  • Leaving construction lines darker than the final outline.
  • Omitting dimensions, view labels or the projection symbol when required.
