COURSE MAP

What this course trains you to do

English Essentials I develops accurate language use and the ability to understand and communicate information. The syllabus groups learning into speaking, listening, reading and drafting skills. Grammar and vocabulary support all four skills; they are not isolated memorisation exercises.

Learning sequence: form correct sentences → choose suitable words → understand spoken and written messages → express ideas clearly → prepare organised paragraphs, emails and letters.

How to study this subject

For every topic, learn the rule, observe a correct example, identify a common error, and then produce your own sentence or short response. Communication marks are earned through clarity, relevance, organisation and correctness—not by using unnecessarily difficult words.

Course identity: EN25C01 English Essentials I is listed in the R-2025 B.E. CSE Semester I curriculum with 2 lecture periods and 2 credits. This page follows the syllabus topics and activities; confirm the course code and prescribed curriculum for your own programme before using it.

1. Grammar foundations

1.1 Parts of speech

Parts of speech describe the function a word performs in a sentence. The same word may perform different functions depending on context.

PartFunctionExample
NounNames a person, place, thing or idea.The engineer checked the machine.
PronounReplaces a noun.She submitted the report.
VerbExpresses action or state.Students analyse the result.
AdjectiveDescribes a noun/pronoun.a precise measurement
AdverbModifies a verb, adjective or adverb.The instrument works accurately.
PrepositionShows relation, often of place, time or direction.The file is on the desk.
ConjunctionConnects words or clauses.Read carefully and answer.
InterjectionExpresses a sudden feeling.Oh! I missed the bus.
Worked identification: “The careful student completed the assignment quickly.” The = article/determiner; careful = adjective; student = noun; completed = verb; the = article/determiner; assignment = noun; quickly = adverb.

1.2 Articles: a, an and the

  • A is used before a singular countable noun beginning with a consonant sound: a university, a book.
  • An is used before a singular countable noun beginning with a vowel sound: an hour, an engineer.
  • The identifies a particular or already-known person or thing: the laboratory near our department.
  • No article is commonly used for plural or uncountable nouns in a general sense: Water is essential. Students need practice.
Remember: choose a/an by sound, not merely by the first written letter. “An honest person” begins with a vowel sound; “a European country” begins with a /y/ consonant sound.

1.3 Tenses

Tense locates an action in time and often shows whether it is simple, continuing, completed or continuing up to a point.

TenseBasic patternExampleTypical use
Present simpleSubject + base verb (s/es for he/she/it)She studies daily.Habit, fact
Present continuousam/is/are + verb-ingShe is studying now.Action in progress
Present perfecthas/have + past participleShe has completed the task.Past action linked to present
Present perfect continuoushas/have been + verb-ingShe has been studying for two hours.Duration continuing to now
Past simplePast formShe studied yesterday.Completed past action
Past continuouswas/were + verb-ingShe was studying at 8 p.m.Action in progress in past
Past perfecthad + past participleShe had left before I arrived.Earlier of two past actions
Future simplewill + base verbShe will study tomorrow.Prediction, decision or future action

Practice rule: identify the time expression and the intended meaning before selecting a tense. “Since” usually introduces a starting point; “for” introduces a duration.

1.4 Sentence structure

A sentence normally contains a subject and a finite verb. The subject tells who or what the sentence is about; the predicate says something about it.

Subject + Verb + Object (SVO): The technician calibrated the instrument.

Other common patterns include Subject + Verb (Birds fly), Subject + Verb + Complement (The result is accurate), and Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object (The teacher gave us an example).

Types of sentences by purpose

  • Declarative: states information — The experiment is complete.
  • Interrogative: asks a question — Is the experiment complete?
  • Imperative: gives an instruction/request — Record the reading.
  • Exclamatory: expresses strong feeling — What a useful result!

Sentence types by clause structure

  • Simple: one independent clause — The motor stopped.
  • Compound: two or more independent clauses joined correctly — The motor stopped, so we checked the supply.
  • Complex: an independent clause and at least one dependent clause — We checked the supply because the motor stopped.
  • Compound-complex: two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

1.5 Subject–verb agreement

The verb must agree with the grammatical subject in person and number. Do not let a nearby noun mislead you.

RuleCorrect example
Singular third-person subject takes -s/-es in present simple.The student writes neatly.
Plural subject takes the base form.The students write neatly.
Two subjects joined by “and” usually take a plural verb.Ravi and Meena are present.
With “either…or / neither…nor”, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject.Neither the teacher nor the students are late.
Words such as each, everyone and somebody are grammatically singular.Everyone has a role.
A phrase between subject and verb does not change the subject's number.The set of tools is missing.
Correction: “The quality of these components are good.” Subject = quality (singular), not components. Correct sentence: “The quality of these components is good.”

1.6 Punctuation and sentence accuracy

  • Use a capital letter at the beginning of a sentence and for proper nouns and the pronoun “I”.
  • Use a full stop for a statement, a question mark for a direct question and an exclamation mark sparingly.
  • Use commas to separate items and to make sentence structure clear; do not join two complete sentences with only a comma.
  • Use an apostrophe for possession or contractions: the student's file; don't.
Frequent error: “Because the test was delayed.” is a dependent clause fragment when written alone. Complete it: “Because the test was delayed, the submission date was extended.”

2.3 Idioms and phrases

An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning may not be obvious from its individual words. Use idioms only when they suit the situation.

  • Break the ice: make people feel comfortable at the start of an interaction.
  • Once in a blue moon: very rarely.
  • Piece of cake: something very easy.
  • Under the weather: feeling unwell.
  • Hit the nail on the head: describe the exact point correctly.
  • In the same boat: in a similar situation.
Exam technique: For a vocabulary question, give the meaning that fits the sentence—not a memorised meaning that changes the context. In your own writing, prefer a familiar precise word over an impressive but incorrect one.

3. Speaking skills

3.1 Self-introduction

A self-introduction should be brief, organised and relevant to the situation. In a classroom or interview-style activity, include your name, place/background if relevant, programme, interests, strengths and a suitable goal.

Good morning. My name is ________. I am studying ________ at ________. I am interested in ________ because ________. I enjoy learning new skills and working with others. My current goal is to improve my knowledge of ________. Thank you.

Delivery: stand comfortably, speak at a moderate pace, pronounce words clearly, make natural eye contact and avoid memorising a speech so rigidly that it sounds unnatural.

3.2 Expressing oneself

PurposeUseful expressions
Giving an opinionIn my opinion… / I believe that… / From my point of view…
AgreeingI agree with that point because… / That is a valid observation.
Partly agreeingI agree to some extent; however…
Disagreeing politelyI understand your view, but I see it differently because…
ClarifyingCould you explain what you mean by…?
Asking for timeLet me think about that for a moment.
ConcludingTo sum up… / The main point is…

3.3 Everyday conversations

A useful conversation has a greeting, a clear purpose, relevant responses and a polite close. Listen to the other person rather than waiting only for your turn.

A: Good morning. Could you tell me where the library is?
B: Good morning. It is on the first floor, next to the computer laboratory.
A: Thank you. Is it open this afternoon?
B: Yes, it is open until 5 p.m.
A: That is helpful. Thank you.
B: You are welcome.

3.4 Team interactions and group discussion

Contribute ideas, support them with reasons or examples, invite quieter members and keep the discussion connected to the topic. Do not interrupt or dismiss another person's view.

  1. Understand the topic and identify the central issue.
  2. Offer one clear point with a reason or example.
  3. Connect your point to what another speaker said.
  4. Ask a focused question when information is unclear.
  5. Summarise the group's agreement and unresolved points.

3.5 Emotions and tone

Choose language that matches the situation. A disagreement can be firm without being rude. “I am concerned about the delay” is more professional than “You are always late.”

3.6 Just-a-minute (JAM) activity

JAM tests organised spontaneous speaking. Use a simple structure: opening statement → two or three connected points → example → closing sentence. If you lose your place, pause briefly and continue; do not fill every silence with “um”.

Sample topic — Importance of time management: “Time management helps students balance classes, assignments and personal responsibilities. Planning tasks by priority reduces last-minute pressure. For example, completing a small part of an assignment each day is easier than doing everything on the final night. Therefore, managing time improves both learning and confidence.”

4. Listening skills

Listening is an active process: receive the sound, recognise words, connect ideas and interpret the speaker's purpose. You do not need to understand every word to understand the main message.

4.1 Listening to conversations, speeches and stories

  1. Before listening: read the task, note the topic and predict the type of information required (names, places, reasons, sequence or opinion).
  2. First listening: identify the speaker, topic, setting and main idea. Avoid stopping mentally at every unfamiliar word.
  3. Second listening: note key facts, examples, numbers, sequence words and changes in opinion.
  4. After listening: check whether the answer matches the question and distinguish facts from assumptions.

4.2 Extracting key information

Use short notes rather than attempting to write every sentence. A useful note format is:

Topic | Main idea | Supporting details | Speaker's attitude | Action / conclusion

4.3 Phonemes and pronunciation awareness

A phoneme is a sound unit that can distinguish one word from another. English spelling does not always map to one predictable sound, so practise listening to and producing sounds rather than relying only on spelling.

  • Listen for vowel contrasts and consonant contrasts that change word meaning.
  • Notice word stress: the syllable made more prominent in a word.
  • Notice sentence stress: important information words are often stressed more strongly.
  • Practise difficult sound pairs slowly, then in short phrases and natural sentences.

4.4 Tone, intent and accents

Tone can indicate certainty, surprise, politeness, frustration, enthusiasm or uncertainty. Interpret tone together with the words and situation. Different English speakers may use different accents, rhythms and vowel sounds; focus first on meaning, then become familiar with variation through regular listening.

Listening practice: listen to a short authentic clip once for the main idea, again for five details, and a third time while checking the transcript if available. Repeat selected sentences aloud to imitate rhythm and stress.

4.5 Gap-fill and spelling activities

For a gap-fill, predict the grammatical category from the surrounding words, listen for the relevant phrase, and check spelling and singular/plural form. In a spelling task, break long words into syllables and check common letter patterns.

5. Reading skills

5.1 Active reading

Active reading means interacting with a text to understand and evaluate it. Before reading, identify the purpose. While reading, mark the main idea, supporting evidence, unfamiliar terms and transitions. After reading, summarise the message in your own words.

5.2 Skimming

Skimming is fast reading to understand the overall topic and structure. Read the title, headings, opening and closing sentences, and repeated key terms. Do not stop to translate every word.

5.3 Scanning

Scanning is searching for a particular detail such as a date, name, number, keyword or definition. Keep the target in mind and move quickly through the page until you find it.

MethodQuestion it answersExample task
SkimmingWhat is this text mainly about?Choose the best title for an article.
ScanningWhere is the specific detail?Find the date of an event.
Close readingWhat exactly does this sentence mean?Infer the writer's reason from a paragraph.

5.4 Reading comprehension method

  1. Read the questions first if the task permits; identify what information is needed.
  2. Read the passage for its main idea.
  3. Locate the relevant sentence or paragraph.
  4. Answer in your own words while preserving the meaning.
  5. For inference questions, combine textual clues; do not add unsupported personal opinions.
  6. Check that each answer directly addresses the question.

5.5 Summarising and note-making

A summary presents only the central ideas in a shorter form. Remove repeated examples and minor details, use your own words, retain the original meaning and avoid adding new claims. Notes can use headings, keywords, arrows and abbreviations; they should remain understandable when revised later.

Mini example: Text idea: “Regular maintenance detects small faults before they become serious failures, improves equipment reliability and can reduce unexpected downtime.”
Notes: Maintenance → early fault detection → reliability ↑ → unexpected downtime ↓.
Summary: Regular maintenance improves reliability by identifying faults early and reducing unplanned downtime.

5.6 Cloze reading and loud reading

In a cloze passage, use grammar, meaning and surrounding context to select the missing word. Read the complete sentence after filling the gap. Loud reading develops fluency and pronunciation: pause at punctuation, group words into meaningful phrases and avoid reading word by word.

6. Drafting and writing skills

6.1 Sentence formation

Start with a complete thought. Check subject, verb, tense, word order and punctuation. Add details only when they improve meaning.

Unclear: “Because the machine was not working.”
Complete: “The experiment was postponed because the machine was not working.”

6.2 Word substitution

Replace wordy or repeated expressions with a precise word, without changing the intended meaning.

Wordy expressionConcise substitution
At this point in timeNow
Due to the fact thatBecause
Make a decisionDecide
Give an explanationExplain
In close proximity toNear

6.3 Developing keywords into a paragraph

Convert keywords into a logical sequence. A paragraph generally has one controlling idea, supporting explanation or examples, and a concluding sentence.

  1. Identify the topic and decide the main point.
  2. Arrange keywords in a logical order (time, cause-effect, problem-solution or comparison).
  3. Write a topic sentence.
  4. Explain the point using relevant facts or examples.
  5. Use linking words where they show a real relationship.
  6. End with a sentence that completes the idea.

6.4 Paragraph structure

Topic: Benefits of reading
Reading is an effective way to improve knowledge and communication. It introduces readers to new ideas, develops vocabulary and strengthens comprehension. Reading newspapers and technical articles also helps students understand current developments in their fields. A regular reading habit therefore supports both academic learning and personal growth.

6.5 Picture and poster interpretation

Describe only what is visible or stated. Identify the central subject, setting, people or objects, activity, message and likely purpose. For a poster, note its headline, intended audience, key instruction, date/place/contact details if present, and visual emphasis. Do not invent missing facts.

6.6 Keywords development

For a topic such as “water conservation”, keywords may be: limited resource, daily use, leakage, rainwater harvesting, reuse, community responsibility. Arrange them into a connected paragraph rather than listing them without explanation.

7. Writing formats

7.1 Official email

A professional email should have a meaningful subject, appropriate greeting, a clear purpose in the opening, necessary details, a polite request or action, and a suitable closing.

To: [Recipient's official email address]
Subject: Request for permission to attend the workshop

Respected Sir/Madam,

I am a student of [Department and Year]. I am writing to request permission to attend the workshop on [topic] scheduled for [date]. The session is relevant to my academic interests and will help me develop practical knowledge.

I kindly request you to grant me permission to participate. I will ensure that any missed academic work is completed.

Thank you for your consideration.

Yours sincerely,
[Name]
[Register number / Class, if required]

7.2 Formal letter

Use a formal letter when a structured written request or communication is required. Include sender's address, date, recipient's designation/address, subject, salutation, body, complimentary close and signature. Follow the format taught by your institution if it differs.

[Sender's address]
[Date]

To
The Principal
[College name and address]

Subject: Request for permission to organise a technical seminar

Respected Sir/Madam,

Our department proposes to organise a technical seminar on [topic] for students on [date]. The programme will provide an opportunity to learn about current developments and interact with resource persons. We request permission to use [venue] and the required facilities.

We assure you that the event will be conducted in an orderly manner. Kindly consider our request.

Thank you.

Yours faithfully,
[Name]
[Class / Department]

7.3 Informal letter

An informal letter is written to a friend or relative. The tone can be warm and personal, but the message should still be organised.

[Your address]
[Date]

Dear [Name],

How are you? I am writing to tell you about the technical exhibition organised at our college. We visited several displays and learned how engineering ideas are used to solve practical problems. My favourite exhibit was ______ because ______. I wish you could have joined us.

Write back when you have time.

With affection,
[Your name]

7.4 Email and letter proofreading checklist

  • Is the recipient and purpose clear?
  • Is the subject line specific and brief?
  • Is the tone suitable—formal or informal?
  • Are dates, names and requested actions accurate?
  • Are paragraphs logically ordered?
  • Have grammar, spelling, punctuation and attachments been checked?

8. Guided practice

8.1 Grammar practice

  1. Choose the correct article: She is ___ honest student.
  2. Change to past simple: “They complete the assignment.”
  3. Choose the correct verb: Each of the machines (need/needs) inspection.
  4. Identify the adverb: “The team worked carefully.”
  5. Join using “although”: “It was raining. The match continued.”
  6. Correct the sentence: “The list of experiments are ready.”
  7. Identify the sentence type: “Please submit the report tomorrow.”

8.2 Vocabulary practice

  1. Give a synonym for “precise” and an antonym for “increase”.
  2. Add a suitable prefix to make the opposite of “possible”.
  3. Form a noun from “kind” using a suffix.
  4. Explain “break the ice” and use it in a sentence.

8.3 Speaking and listening tasks

  1. Prepare a one-minute self-introduction for a classroom activity.
  2. Speak for one minute on “A skill I want to develop”.
  3. In a group of four, discuss whether students should participate in more technical clubs. Each member should contribute a reason and respond politely to another view.
  4. Listen to a short speech and record its topic, main idea, three supporting details and the speaker's tone.
  5. Listen to two speakers with different accents and note the key message without judging the accent.

8.4 Reading and writing tasks

  1. Skim a newspaper article and write its main idea in one sentence.
  2. Scan the same article to locate one date, one name and one numerical detail.
  3. Write a 100–120 word paragraph on “The importance of teamwork”.
  4. Draft an official email requesting information about an academic event.
  5. Interpret a public-awareness poster in five complete sentences.
  6. Read a long passage and prepare notes with headings and sub-points.

8.5 Answer key — grammar and vocabulary

  1. an
  2. They completed the assignment.
  3. needs
  4. carefully
  5. Although it was raining, the match continued.
  6. The list of experiments is ready.
  7. Imperative sentence.
  8. Precise: accurate/exact; increase: decrease/reduce.
  9. im- → impossible.
  10. kindness.
  11. To make people feel comfortable at the beginning of an interaction. Example: “The host asked a simple question to break the ice.”

Open-ended speaking, listening, reading and writing activities should be assessed using relevance, clarity, organisation, accuracy and completion of the task.

9.2 How to prepare for an examination

  • Revise grammar rules with your own examples, not definitions alone.
  • Practise sentence correction, tense selection, articles and subject–verb agreement.
  • Build vocabulary through contextual sentences and word families.
  • Practise short speaking tasks aloud and time yourself.
  • For listening, practise identifying the main idea and details from a single playback.
  • For reading, distinguish skimming, scanning and close reading.
  • For writing, learn the structure of paragraphs, official emails and formal/informal letters.
  • Reserve time to proofread every written answer.

9.3 Writing answer-quality checklist

Content
Relevant, complete and accurate response to the task.
Organisation
Logical order, clear paragraphs and suitable linking words.
Language
Correct grammar, vocabulary, spelling and punctuation.
Presentation
Readable layout, appropriate tone and required format.

9.4 Last-minute revision sheet

  • Grammar: parts of speech; articles by sound and context; tense forms; sentence patterns; sentence types; subject–verb agreement.
  • Vocabulary: synonyms, antonyms, prefixes, suffixes, idioms and phrases.
  • Speaking: self-introduction, expressing opinions, everyday conversation, team interaction, emotions, agreement and polite disagreement, JAM.
  • Listening: simple conversations, short speeches/stories, key information, phonemes, tone, intent and accents.
  • Reading: skimming, scanning, active reading, summarising, loud reading, cloze reading, comprehension and note-making.
  • Writing: sentence formation, word substitution, keyword development, paragraphs, picture/poster interpretation, emails and letters.
Exam answer principle: Answer the exact task. For a format-based question, use the correct format; for a comprehension question, use evidence from the passage; for a speaking or writing task, communicate one clear message with suitable tone and correct language.
