Purpose of Makerspace

Makerspace develops the ability to understand how products are built, identify their parts, use tools correctly, make simple joints, recognise electrical and electronic components and integrate basic sensors and actuators. Students learn through supervised practical work, demonstrations, teamwork and documentation.

A · Assembly
Tools, appliances, air-conditioners, refrigerators and bicycle.
B · Welding
Welding selection, preparation, arc power sources and supervised weld practice.
C · Electrical wiring
Installation tools, protective devices, lighting, fans, bells, motors and UPS.
D · Electronics
Components, computer assembly, PCB soldering, rectifier, op-amp, sensors and PLC.

Course overview and learning outcomes

ME25C04 is a laboratory course with four practical contact periods per week and two credits in the B.E. Mechanical Engineering R-2025 curriculum. The curriculum gives two broad objectives: practical competence in assembly, disassembly and welding; and hands-on exposure to electrical wiring, electronic components, sensors and actuators.

Expected student abilities

  • Identify common hand tools and select them for an appropriate task.
  • Follow a controlled sequence for disassembly, inspection and reassembly.
  • Recognise welding processes and prepare a simple joint under instructor supervision.
  • Identify electrical installation devices and explain their protective purpose.
  • Recognise common electronic components and make a safe soldered PCB joint.
  • Explain the basic relationship between a sensor, controller and actuator.
  • Maintain a clear practical record and communicate observations.
Course scope note: The curriculum lists activities rather than a fixed numbered experiment list. Your college may arrange these activities into a different number of lab sessions. Use your department's approved experiment list for the final record index.

How to write a Makerspace practical record

Use the following structure for each activity unless your faculty has prescribed a different format.

EXPERIMENT NO.: ________ DATE: __________ TITLE: AIM: TOOLS / EQUIPMENT / COMPONENTS REQUIRED: THEORY / WORKING PRINCIPLE: PROCEDURE: OBSERVATIONS: DIAGRAM / COMPONENT IDENTIFICATION: RESULT: PRECAUTIONS / SAFETY: VIVA QUESTIONS:

What makes a good record?

  • Write the actual tool and component names, not just “required materials”.
  • Draw a neat labelled diagram of the assembly, joint or circuit only after confirming it with the lab model.
  • Record the actual observation; never invent a measurement or claim a test passed if it was not performed.
  • Write the sequence in the order you performed it.
  • Include safety precautions specific to the activity.
Sample result wording: “The components were identified, inspected and reassembled under supervision. The assembly was checked for correct fit and free movement.” Change this statement to match the actual task and result.

1.3 General tool-handling procedure

  1. Identify the job and read the equipment or appliance instructions.
  2. Select the correct tool and inspect it for damage, loose handles, worn tips or cracked insulation.
  3. Secure the workpiece before applying force.
  4. Keep hands clear of pinch points and cutting edges.
  5. Use eye protection when chips, dust or fragments may be produced.
  6. Do not extend a tool handle with an unauthorised pipe or improvised lever.
  7. Clean, count and return tools after the activity.

2. Disassembly and assembly practices

2.1 Standard sequence for any appliance

Electrical isolation is mandatory: Before opening any mains-powered appliance, disconnect it from the supply, verify isolation using the lab's approved method and follow the instructor's procedure. Capacitors and other components may retain hazardous energy after unplugging. Refrigeration systems contain pressurised refrigerant and must only be opened or serviced by trained authorised personnel.
  1. Identify: note appliance name, model, rating plate and major external parts.
  2. Inspect: look for damage, missing fasteners, corrosion, loose parts or damaged cables.
  3. Isolate: disconnect all energy sources; instructor verifies safe condition.
  4. Document: take an approved reference photo or sketch and label connectors/parts before removal.
  5. Disassemble: remove covers and fasteners in a controlled sequence using correct tools; store parts in labelled trays.
  6. Study: identify the function of each component and note its mounting and connection method.
  7. Reassemble: reverse the documented sequence, check alignment, correct fasteners and cable routing.
  8. Inspect: check that no tools or loose parts remain and that guards/covers are restored.
  9. Test: only the instructor or authorised person performs any powered test after safety checks.

2.2 Mixer grinder

Main parts to identify: base housing, electric motor, motor shaft, cooling openings, coupling, jar, blade assembly, lid, speed selector and supply cord. Construction varies by model.

Working principle: electrical energy drives a motor. The rotating shaft transfers motion through a coupling to the jar blade, which cuts or mixes the contents. Ventilation openings help remove motor heat.

Study points during disassembly: identify the motor mounting, coupling arrangement, jar locking feature, switch and cord entry. Do not energise the exposed assembly. Blade edges are sharp; handle the jar assembly carefully.

2.3 Ceiling fan and table fan

Major parts: blades, hub, motor stator, rotor, bearings/bushes, shaft, capacitor where used, regulator/control arrangement, housing and protective covers. Ceiling fans also include downrod, canopy and suspension hardware; table fans include base, oscillation mechanism and guards.

Working principle: the motor produces torque that rotates the fan blades. The blades impart momentum to air and create airflow. Bearings support the rotating shaft; the capacitor and winding arrangement depend on the motor design.

Inspection points: blade condition and balance, bearing play, shaft condition, cable insulation, terminal condition, guard integrity and secure fasteners. Never run a fan with its guard removed or with loose blades.

2.4 Washing machine

Major systems: outer cabinet, wash drum/tub, motor or drive system, belt or direct-drive arrangement, water inlet valve, drain pump, hoses, suspension/dampers, control board, door/lid interlock and wiring harness.

Working principle: the machine controls water intake, drum motion, washing/rinsing and water removal. The controller coordinates the motor, valves, pump and safety interlocks according to the selected programme.

Study points: identify the water path, drain path, drum support, vibration isolation and interlock. Do not disconnect hoses or open water-bearing components without instructor approval; check for residual water and isolate the appliance.

2.5 Air-conditioner and refrigerator

Refrigeration cycle components: compressor, condenser, expansion device and evaporator. Refrigerant circulates through the system and transfers heat from the cooled space to the surroundings.

Air-conditioner additional parts: indoor and outdoor heat exchangers, fans, filters, control electronics, sensors, drain arrangement and refrigerant piping.

Refrigerator additional parts: insulated cabinet, evaporator compartment, condenser arrangement, thermostat/controller, door seal and storage compartments.

Restricted service work: Students may identify components and study a cutaway/demo unit. Do not cut, loosen, puncture, vent, recover, charge or braze refrigerant lines as an ordinary Makerspace exercise. Refrigerant handling and sealed-system repair require trained personnel, correct recovery equipment and applicable environmental/safety procedures.

2.6 Bicycle assembly and disassembly

Main parts: frame, fork, handlebar, stem, saddle, seat post, wheels, hubs, tyres, tubes, spokes, rims, crankset, pedals, chain, sprockets, derailleurs, brake levers, cables and brake calipers or disc brakes.

Inspection: check tyre condition and inflation, wheel security, chain condition and lubrication, brake operation, steering movement, pedal/crank security and frame damage.

  1. Place the bicycle in a stable work stand.
  2. Identify the main assemblies and note the position of spacers, washers and fasteners.
  3. Use suitable tools to remove only the components specified by the instructor.
  4. Inspect threads, bearings, chain, brake pads and moving parts.
  5. Reassemble in the correct orientation and tighten using manufacturer-specified torque where available.
  6. Check steering, wheel rotation, gear operation and both brakes before any riding test.
Important: A bicycle must not be ridden after assembly until the instructor has checked wheel retention, steering and braking. Incorrect brake or wheel installation can cause serious injury.

3. Welding practices

3.1 What is welding?

Welding is a joining process in which materials are joined by producing coalescence, usually through heat, pressure or both, with or without filler material. The selected process depends on base material, thickness, joint design, production rate, quality requirement and working environment.

3.2 Joint types and edge preparation

JointDescriptionCommon application
Butt jointTwo members lie approximately in the same plane and meet edge-to-edge.Plates, pipes and sheet assemblies.
Lap jointOne member overlaps another.Sheet-metal fabrication.
T-jointOne member meets another approximately at right angles.Frames and brackets.
Corner jointMembers meet at a corner.Boxes and enclosures.
Edge jointAdjacent edges are joined.Suitable thin-sheet applications.

Joint preparation may include cutting, cleaning, removing oil/paint/rust, aligning and preparing a groove. Groove design depends on thickness, process, access and required penetration. Use the drawing or welding procedure supplied by the instructor; do not guess groove angle or root gap.

3.3 Welding process overview

ProcessPrincipleTypical features
MAW / SMAWArc between a flux-coated consumable electrode and workpiece; flux forms shielding and slag.Portable, versatile; electrode changes and slag removal are needed.
GMAW / MIG-MAGContinuous consumable wire electrode with externally supplied shielding gas.High deposition rate; shielding gas can be affected by wind.
GTAW / TIGNon-consumable tungsten electrode with shielding gas; filler may be added separately.Precise, clean welds; generally slower and requires operator skill.

MAW terminology: Many teaching manuals use MAW for Manual Arc Welding and SMAW for Shielded Metal Arc Welding. Confirm the abbreviation used by your laboratory manual.

3.4 Welding power source

An arc-welding power source supplies controlled electrical energy to establish and maintain an arc. Depending on the process, equipment may provide constant-current or constant-voltage characteristics. GMAW commonly uses a constant-voltage power source with wire-feed control; GTAW commonly uses a constant-current source. The correct source, polarity and settings are selected from the approved procedure and equipment instructions.

3.5 General supervised welding workflow

  1. Read the job drawing and identify base material, thickness, joint type and process.
  2. Instructor confirms the approved welding procedure, machine condition, settings, consumable and shielding arrangement.
  3. Prepare the base material and joint groove as specified; remove contaminants and verify fit-up.
  4. Clamp and position the work securely using suitable fixtures.
  5. Wear the specified welding helmet with correct shade, gloves, flame-resistant clothing, safety footwear and other required PPE.
  6. Check screens, ventilation, fire protection, cable condition, earth/work return connection and gas-cylinder security.
  7. Perform the weld only under direct instructor supervision in the designated welding bay.
  8. Allow the work to cool safely; remove slag only with suitable tools and eye protection.
  9. Visually inspect the weld for continuity, shape, obvious defects and dimensional conformity.
  10. Record process, material, joint, consumable and observations as actually used.
Welding safety: Never look at an arc without the correct welding filter. Protect nearby people from arc flash with screens. Keep flammable materials away, ensure ventilation, avoid wet conditions, and never touch live electrode parts. Gas cylinders must be secured and handled by trained personnel. Hot metal remains dangerous after the arc stops.

3.6 Common weld discontinuities

DiscontinuityGeneral description
PorosityGas cavities trapped in the solidified weld metal.
UndercutGroove melted at the weld toe and not adequately filled.
Lack of fusionWeld metal does not properly fuse with base metal or previous pass.
Incomplete penetrationWeld metal does not extend through the intended joint root.

Visual inspection can identify some surface conditions but cannot confirm internal soundness. Use only the inspection method required by the instructor or applicable standard.

4.2 Protective devices

Fuse: a sacrificial overcurrent protection device. Its element melts when current exceeds the specified time-current characteristic, interrupting the circuit.

MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker): a resettable protective device that trips under specified overload and short-circuit conditions. Its rating and tripping characteristic must suit the circuit and cable.

RCCB/RCD: detects residual current imbalance and disconnects a circuit to reduce electric-shock risk. It does not replace overcurrent protection unless it is an RCBO or combined protective arrangement.

Distribution board: houses protective devices and distributes supply to outgoing circuits. Correct circuit identification, enclosure, earthing, conductor sizing and coordination are essential.

Important distinction: A fuse or MCB primarily protects against overcurrent. An RCD/RCCB responds to residual-current imbalance. The devices perform different protective functions.

4.3 Basic wiring activity: lighting, fan and calling bell

The syllabus includes supervised connections for lighting, fans and calling bells. Students should identify the load, switch, protective device, neutral and protective-earth arrangements shown in the approved training board diagram.

Live mains wiring is not a do-it-yourself exercise. This page does not provide terminal-by-terminal live wiring instructions. All connections must be made on an isolated training board by students under qualified faculty supervision, then inspected and tested by the authorised instructor before power is applied. Never work on household mains circuits independently.

4.4 Motor and UPS overview

Motor connection activity: identify the motor nameplate, rated voltage, phase, frequency, power, current, speed and connection diagram. Explain the purpose of the starter/control arrangement and overload protection. The exact connection depends on motor type and nameplate.

UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply): provides temporary power when the main supply fails. A typical UPS includes a rectifier/charger, battery, inverter, control circuit and transfer arrangement. A UPS may contain hazardous stored energy even after it is unplugged.

4.5 Electrical safety checklist

  • Only authorised persons operate the supply isolation and energisation controls.
  • Use lockout/tagout or the institution's approved isolation system.
  • Verify absence of voltage with an approved tester and method.
  • Use correct protective devices, cable size, enclosure and earthing as designed.
  • Keep hands and floor dry; remove jewellery and loose items.
  • Never bypass a fuse, MCB, interlock or protective earth.
  • Do not use a damaged lead, plug, socket or instrument.
  • Keep the emergency switch accessible and know the lab's emergency procedure.

5.2 Electronic test equipment

  • Digital multimeter: measures voltage, current, resistance and continuity within its rating.
  • Oscilloscope: displays voltage versus time and helps examine waveform shape, amplitude, period and noise.
  • Bench power supply: provides controlled DC voltage/current with current limiting.
  • Function generator: produces test waveforms such as sine, square and triangle signals.
  • Soldering station: heats a joint so solder can wet the pad and component lead.

5.3 Computer disassembly and assembly

Major parts: cabinet/chassis, motherboard, CPU, CPU cooler, RAM, storage drive, power supply, expansion cards, fans, front-panel connectors and data/power cables.

  1. Shut down the computer, disconnect external supply and peripherals, and follow the lab's ESD procedure.
  2. Use an ESD-safe work surface and wrist strap where specified; handle boards by their edges.
  3. Document cable locations and component orientation before removal.
  4. Remove the cover and identify the motherboard, memory, storage, power supply and cooling system.
  5. Remove only the components authorised for the activity; do not open the power-supply enclosure.
  6. Inspect connectors and components for damage, dust or corrosion.
  7. Reinstall parts in the correct slots/orientation and reconnect documented cables.
  8. Instructor checks the assembly before power is restored.
ESD and power-supply safety: Static discharge can damage electronic components. A computer power supply can contain hazardous stored voltage. Do not open its metal enclosure. Isolate mains power and follow the college's ESD and handling procedures.

5.4 PCB soldering practice

Soldering joins a component lead to a printed-circuit-board pad using a suitable solder alloy and controlled heat. A good joint has proper wetting, a sound mechanical connection and no unintended bridges to adjacent pads.

  1. Identify the PCB, component, polarity, pad locations and soldering instructions.
  2. Inspect the soldering iron tip and set the station to the approved temperature for the solder and component.
  3. Secure the board and position the component correctly.
  4. Heat the pad and lead together, then feed an appropriate amount of solder to the joint—not directly as a blob onto the iron.
  5. Remove solder feed and iron smoothly; allow the joint to cool without movement.
  6. Inspect for wetting, shape, bridges, insufficient solder and disturbed joints.
  7. Clean residues only by the method specified for the solder/flux and lab.
Soldering safety: Use fume extraction, eye protection and a stable iron stand. The tip is hot enough to cause burns. Wash hands after handling solder; follow the lab's rules for lead-containing solder and waste disposal.

5.5 Bridge rectifier

A bridge rectifier uses four diodes to convert both half-cycles of an AC input into a pulsating DC output. During each half-cycle, a pair of diodes conducts so that load current flows in the same direction.

Waveform concept: AC input alternates positive and negative. Full-wave rectification flips the negative half-cycle to the output polarity. A filter capacitor can reduce ripple, but the output is not perfectly constant without suitable filtering and regulation.

5.6 Operational amplifier (Op-Amp)

An operational amplifier is a high-gain differential voltage amplifier. It has inverting (−) and non-inverting (+) inputs and an output. With negative feedback and suitable operating conditions, it can be configured for amplification, buffering, summing, filtering and other signal-processing tasks.

ConfigurationIdeal closed-loop relationKey idea
Inverting amplifierAv = −Rf/RinOutput is inverted relative to input.
Non-inverting amplifierAv = 1 + Rf/R1Output is in phase with input.
Voltage followerAv ≈ 1Buffer with high input and low output impedance.

Real op-amps have finite bandwidth, input offset, output-current limits and output-voltage swing limits. The supply voltage and pin configuration must be checked in the datasheet.

5.7 Transimpedance amplifier

A transimpedance amplifier (TIA) converts a small input current into an output voltage, commonly using an op-amp with a feedback resistor. Photodiodes are a common current-output sensor used with a TIA.

Ideal inverting TIA relation: Vout ≈ −Iin × Rf Iin = input current (A) Rf = feedback resistance (Ω) Vout = output voltage (V)

In practice, feedback capacitance, sensor capacitance, noise, bandwidth and op-amp stability must be considered. The output is limited by the available supply rails.

5.8 Sensors and actuators with a microcontroller

A sensor measures a physical quantity and produces a signal. A microcontroller reads the signal, executes a program and generates a control output. An actuator converts that output into a physical action.

Sensor examples
Temperature, light, proximity, pressure, humidity, position and motion sensors.
Actuator examples
LED, buzzer, relay module, motor driver, servo and solenoid.

System flow: physical quantity → sensor → signal conditioning / input interface → microcontroller → driver stage → actuator → physical response.

Never connect a motor, relay coil or other high-current load directly to a microcontroller I/O pin unless the board specification explicitly permits it. Use a suitable driver, protection and common-reference arrangement as designed by the instructor.

5.9 Programmable Logic Controller (PLC)

A PLC is an industrial controller designed to operate reliably in automation environments. It reads input signals, executes a user programme and updates output devices.

PLC elementFunction
Power supplyProvides regulated power to the controller and modules.
Input moduleReceives signals from switches and sensors.
CPU / processorExecutes the control programme.
Output moduleControls indicators, contactors, valves or other loads through suitable interfaces.
Programming deviceUsed to develop, download, monitor and troubleshoot the programme.

A common PLC scan includes reading inputs, executing the logic and updating outputs. The actual controller architecture and scan behaviour depend on the model.

5.10 PLC demonstration: simple motor-start logic concept

For a demonstration, the instructor may show how a start input, stop input and overload status are used to control a motor starter output. The programme should include a safe stop condition and overload trip. The actual motor power circuit must use correctly rated protective and switching equipment; a PLC output is not itself a motor power supply.

7. Viva voce questions and answers

  1. What is Makerspace?
    A practical learning environment for making, assembling, testing and understanding products using tools and technologies.
  2. Why should tools be inspected before use?
    To identify damage that could cause poor work or injury.
  3. What is the purpose of a bench vice?
    To hold a workpiece securely during an operation.
  4. Why label parts during disassembly?
    To preserve their identity, position and orientation for correct reassembly.
  5. What is the function of a bearing?
    To support relative motion and reduce friction while carrying loads.
  6. What is the function of a capacitor in some fan motors?
    It provides a phase shift needed for the motor's starting or running characteristics, depending on the design.
  7. Name the four main components of a vapour-compression system.
    Compressor, condenser, expansion device and evaporator.
  8. What is welding?
    A joining process that produces coalescence using heat, pressure or both, with or without filler.
  9. What is the difference between GMAW and GTAW?
    GMAW uses a continuously fed consumable wire electrode; GTAW uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode.
  10. What is a welding heat-affected zone?
    The base-metal region whose microstructure or properties are changed by welding heat but which has not melted.
  11. What is porosity?
    Gas cavities trapped in weld metal.
  12. Why is shielding gas used?
    To protect the weld pool and hot electrode region from atmospheric contamination.
  13. What is a fuse?
    An overcurrent protective device that interrupts a circuit by melting its element under specified conditions.
  14. What is an MCB?
    A resettable circuit breaker that trips under specified overload or short-circuit conditions.
  15. Does an MCB provide the same function as an RCCB?
    No. An MCB responds to overcurrent; an RCCB responds to residual-current imbalance.
  16. What is earthing?
    A protective connection to earth intended to help control dangerous touch voltages and support fault protection.
  17. Why must a circuit be isolated before continuity testing?
    Continuity mode is not intended for energised circuits; testing live can damage the meter and injure the user.
  18. What is the function of a resistor?
    It opposes current flow and is used to limit current, divide voltage and set circuit conditions.
  19. What is a diode?
    A semiconductor device that conducts preferentially in one direction.
  20. What is a bridge rectifier?
    A four-diode arrangement that performs full-wave rectification.
  21. What is an op-amp?
    A high-gain differential amplifier used with feedback in many analogue circuits.
  22. What is a transimpedance amplifier?
    An amplifier that converts input current into output voltage.
  23. What is a sensor?
    A device that detects a physical quantity and produces a corresponding signal.
  24. What is an actuator?
    A device that converts a control signal or energy input into physical action.
  25. What is a PLC?
    A programmable industrial controller that reads inputs, executes logic and updates outputs.
  26. Why is ESD protection required in computer assembly?
    Electrostatic discharge can damage sensitive electronic components.
  27. Why should a soldering iron be kept in its stand?
    To prevent burns, fire and accidental contact with the hot tip.
  28. What is the purpose of a UPS?
    To provide temporary backup power when the main supply is interrupted.

8. Practical examination checklist

Before entering the laboratory

  • Wear the PPE required for the station: safety shoes, coat, goggles, gloves or welding helmet as instructed.
  • Tie back long hair and remove loose clothing and jewellery.
  • Read the activity sheet and know the emergency stop / isolation location.
  • Identify the tools and equipment before starting.

During the practical

  • Confirm instructor approval before operating equipment.
  • Keep the work area organised and dry where electrical equipment is used.
  • Do not bypass guards, interlocks or protective devices.
  • Record the actual procedure and observations.
  • Ask before handling unfamiliar, hot, sharp, pressurised or energised components.

At the end

  • Switch off and isolate equipment as directed.
  • Return tools and unused components.
  • Dispose of metal, electronic and solder waste in the designated bins.
  • Clean the bench and obtain faculty sign-off.

Common practical mistakes

  • Mixing fasteners from different appliance covers.
  • Forcing a connector or component in the wrong orientation.
  • Using the wrong spanner or screwdriver size.
  • Failing to record cable routing before disassembly.
  • Confusing fuse/MCB protection with residual-current protection.
  • Creating solder bridges or cold joints through poor heating technique.
  • Connecting a load directly to a microcontroller pin without a suitable driver.
  • Applying power before the instructor completes the safety inspection.
Practical exam strategy: State the aim, identify tools and components, explain the principle briefly, demonstrate the safe sequence, describe what you observed and state the result. Examiners often assess safe handling and understanding—not just whether the assembly appears to work.
