1. Properties of matter — elasticity

1.1 Elastic and plastic behaviour

When an external force is applied to a body, its shape or dimensions may change. Elastic deformation disappears when the force is removed, provided the material remains within its elastic limit. Plastic deformation remains after unloading. Real materials may show both behaviours depending on load, temperature and loading history.

1.2 Stress and strain

Stress is the internal restoring force per unit area developed in a body under loading. Strain is the ratio of change in dimension to original dimension and is dimensionless.

Normal stress: σ = F/A    |    Longitudinal strain: ε = ΔL/L

SI unit of stress is pascal (Pa = N m⁻²). Strain has no unit. Tensile stress tends to lengthen a body; compressive stress tends to shorten it.

1.3 Hooke's law and Young's modulus

Within the proportional limit, stress is directly proportional to strain. The constant of proportionality for a linearly elastic material under uniaxial loading is Young's modulus.

Young's modulus: E = longitudinal stress / longitudinal strain = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = FL/(AΔL)

A larger E means the material is stiffer in tension or compression. It does not automatically mean the material is stronger; stiffness and strength are different properties.

1.4 Other elastic moduli

ModulusDefinitionExpression
Young's modulus ELongitudinal stress / longitudinal strainσ/ε
Bulk modulus KHydrostatic pressure change / volumetric strain (with sign convention)K = −Δp/(ΔV/V)
Rigidity modulus GShear stress / shear strainτ/γ

For an isotropic, linearly elastic material: E=2G(1+ν) and E=3K(1−2ν), where ν is Poisson's ratio. These relations apply under the assumptions of homogeneous, isotropic linear elasticity.

1.5 Poisson's ratio

When a rod is stretched longitudinally, its lateral dimensions usually decrease. Poisson's ratio is the negative ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain:

ν = −(lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain)

2. Cantilever and Young's modulus by non-uniform bending

2.1 Cantilever

A cantilever is a beam rigidly fixed at one end and free at the other. When a load is applied at the free end, the beam bends. Examples include balconies, projecting shelves, crane arms and diving boards.

Rigid wall / fixed end━━━━━━Free end + load W ↓

The fixed end has zero displacement and zero rotation in the ideal model.

2.2 Non-uniform bending of a cantilever

For a rectangular cantilever of breadth b and thickness d, length L, carrying a point load W at its free end, the depression at the free end under small-deflection Euler–Bernoulli beam assumptions is:

y = WL³/(3EI), where I = bd³/12

Therefore:

E = 4WL³/(bd³y)

Here W is force in newtons, L is beam length, b is breadth, d is thickness, y is measured depression and E is in pascals when SI units are used. The thickness is cubed; even a small error in d can significantly affect the calculated modulus.

Illustrative calculation: Suppose W=2 N, L=0.50 m, b=0.025 m, d=0.005 m and y=0.010 m. Then E=4(2)(0.50³)/[0.025(0.005³)(0.010)] = 3.2×10¹¹ Pa. This is only a calculation demonstration; real experimental values must use measured data and carefully verified dimensions.

2.3 Why it is called non-uniform bending

For an end-loaded cantilever, bending moment varies along the length: it is greatest at the fixed end and reduces towards the free end. Curvature therefore varies along the beam. This differs from a beam region under constant bending moment.

2.4 Experimental outline

  1. Measure the length, breadth and thickness of the beam at suitable positions.
  2. Clamp one end firmly and keep the beam horizontal without load.
  3. Place the load hanger at the specified free-end position.
  4. Record the initial reference reading and then the depression for known loads.
  5. Repeat readings while loading and unloading where instructed.
  6. Plot load W against depression y, or use the graph method specified by the laboratory manual.
  7. Calculate E using the verified formula and SI units.
Precautions: avoid jerking the load; do not exceed the elastic range; ensure the clamp does not slip; read the scale without parallax; measure thickness accurately; and allow oscillations to settle before taking a reading.

3. Girders in bridges and buildings

A girder is a major horizontal structural member that supports loads from smaller beams, slabs or other structural elements and transfers them to supports. It is designed to resist bending, shear and sometimes torsion.

3.1 Bending of a beam

When a beam bends, one side is in compression and the opposite side is in tension. Between them is a neutral surface; its intersection with a cross-section is the neutral axis, where longitudinal bending stress is zero in the elementary beam model.

Top fibres: compression↓Neutral axis: zero longitudinal stress↓Bottom fibres: tension

3.2 Flexural rigidity

The resistance to bending depends on material stiffness E and the second moment of area I. Their product EI is called flexural rigidity.

Bending equation: M/I = σ/y = E/R

M is bending moment, I is second moment of area about the neutral axis, σ is bending stress at distance y from the neutral axis, E is Young's modulus and R is radius of curvature. This is the elementary bending equation for a slender beam under the assumptions of simple bending.

3.3 Why I-sections are common

For a given amount of material, placing more material far from the neutral axis increases the second moment of area. An I-section has wide flanges separated by a web: flanges efficiently resist tensile and compressive bending stresses, while the web carries much of the shear force. Actual structural design also checks buckling, local instability, connections, fatigue, deflection and applicable design codes.

3.4 Applications

  • Bridge girders support deck loads and transfer them to piers or abutments.
  • Building beams and girders carry floor and roof loads.
  • Crane girders support moving hoists and trolley loads.
  • Machine frames and bed structures require adequate bending stiffness.

4. Viscosity — Stokes' method

4.1 Meaning of viscosity

Viscosity is the internal resistance of a fluid to relative motion between adjacent layers. A high-viscosity liquid flows less readily than a low-viscosity liquid under comparable conditions. Dynamic viscosity η has SI unit Pa·s.

4.2 Newton's law of viscosity

τ = η (du/dy)

τ is shear stress and du/dy is the velocity gradient perpendicular to the direction of flow. For a Newtonian fluid, shear stress is proportional to velocity gradient under the stated conditions.

4.3 Stokes' law

For a small sphere moving slowly through a viscous fluid under laminar-flow conditions, the viscous drag is:

Fᵥ = 6πηrv

r is sphere radius and v is its speed relative to the fluid. Stokes' law assumes a small Reynolds number and a sphere sufficiently far from container boundaries.

4.4 Terminal velocity method

A sphere falling through a liquid first accelerates. As speed increases, viscous drag increases. Eventually the downward effective weight is balanced by upward buoyancy and viscous drag; acceleration becomes zero and the sphere reaches terminal velocity.

At terminal speed: (4/3)πr³(ρₛ−ρₗ)g = 6πηrvₜ
η = [2r²g(ρₛ−ρₗ)]/(9vₜ)

ρₛ is sphere density, ρₗ is liquid density and g is acceleration due to gravity. Measure vₜ over a known distance after the sphere has reached steady speed.

Illustration: If a sphere travels 0.20 m in 4.0 s at terminal speed, vₜ=0.20/4.0=0.050 m s⁻¹. Substitute this value with the measured radius and densities to calculate η.

4.5 Experimental steps and precautions

  1. Measure the sphere diameter with a micrometer screw gauge and calculate radius.
  2. Determine the sphere and liquid densities using the prescribed method.
  3. Fill a tall transparent tube with the test liquid and mark a measured distance away from the entry region.
  4. Release a clean sphere gently along the central axis.
  5. Measure travel time between the marks after terminal speed is reached.
  6. Repeat for several trials and spheres if specified; calculate mean terminal velocity and viscosity.

Keep the liquid temperature stable because viscosity changes with temperature. Avoid bubbles, wall contact and timing before terminal speed. Apply wall corrections only if the laboratory manual specifies them.

5. Surface tension — drop weight method

Surface tension is the tangential force per unit length acting at a liquid surface, normal to an imaginary line drawn on the surface. It can also be understood as surface energy per unit area. SI unit: N m⁻¹.

5.1 Molecular explanation

Molecules inside a liquid experience attractions in all directions, approximately balanced overall. Molecules at the free surface have a different neighbourhood and experience a net inward attraction. The surface tends to contract and minimise its area, subject to constraints.

5.2 Drop formation and drop weight method

In the drop weight method, liquid drops detach from the end of a capillary or burette when their weight becomes sufficient to overcome the surface-tension force around the rim. Under idealised conditions, the drop weight is related to surface tension by a force balance.

Idealised relation: mg ≈ 2πrγ

m is mass of a detached drop, r is the radius of the tip and γ is surface tension. Real drops do not necessarily detach at the exact ideal force-balance size; a correction factor may be required depending on the apparatus and method. Follow the formula and correction prescribed in your lab manual.

5.3 Comparative drop method

When comparing a test liquid with a reference liquid using the same apparatus, the tip geometry cancels in the ratio. Under the same conditions and with the same number of drops, surface tension is proportional to mass per drop:

γ₁/γ₂ = (m₁/n₁)/(m₂/n₂)

For equal drop counts, this becomes γ₁/γ₂=m₁/m₂. Use density and drop-count corrections if the specific experimental method requires them.

5.4 Factors affecting surface tension

  • Temperature: surface tension generally decreases as temperature rises.
  • Impurities and dissolved substances can increase or decrease it depending on the substance.
  • Surfactants reduce surface tension and improve wetting.

5.5 Applications

Capillary action, liquid drops, sprays, detergents, inkjet printing, wetting and coating processes are influenced by surface tension.

6. Thermal expansion, thermal stress and bimetallic strips

6.1 Thermal expansion

Most materials expand when heated because the average separation between atoms or molecules increases. The amount of expansion depends on material, initial dimensions and temperature change.

Linear expansion: ΔL = αL₀ΔT
Area expansion: ΔA ≈ βA₀ΔT; for isotropic solids β ≈ 2α
Volume expansion: ΔV = γᵥV₀ΔT; for isotropic solids γᵥ ≈ 3α

α is the coefficient of linear expansion in K⁻¹; β and γᵥ are area and volume expansion coefficients. Temperature differences in °C and K have the same numerical magnitude.

Example: A 2 m metal bar has α=12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ and is heated by 50 K. ΔL=12×10⁻⁶×2×50=0.0012 m=1.2 mm.

6.2 Thermal stress

If a bar is free to expand, thermal expansion produces a dimensional change but no thermal stress in the ideal case. If expansion is fully prevented, a stress develops.

For complete restraint: σ = EαΔT

The magnitude is tensile for prevented cooling contraction and compressive for prevented heating expansion. The sign convention should be stated. This ideal equation assumes uniform temperature, linear elasticity and complete restraint.

Example: For E=200 GPa, α=12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ and ΔT=40 K, the magnitude of fully restrained thermal stress is 200×10⁹×12×10⁻⁶×40=96 MPa.

6.3 Bimetallic strip

A bimetallic strip consists of two different metals bonded together. Since they have different coefficients of thermal expansion, heating causes one layer to expand more than the other. Because they are bonded, the strip bends. On heating, it bends towards the metal with the lower expansion coefficient (for the usual simple strip arrangement).

Metal A: higher αbondedMetal B: lower α

On heating, the higher-expansion side becomes the longer outer side; the strip curves towards the lower-expansion side.

6.4 Expansion joints

Expansion joints provide space for thermal movement in bridges, railway tracks, pipelines, concrete slabs and long building structures. Without suitable allowance, restrained expansion can cause large stresses, buckling, cracking or damage to supports. Design must consider temperature range, movement, fatigue, sealing and structural loading.

7. Oscillations — simple harmonic motion and torsional pendulum

7.1 Periodic motion and oscillation

Periodic motion repeats after equal time intervals. An oscillation is a to-and-fro motion about an equilibrium position. The time for one complete cycle is the period T; frequency f=1/T; angular frequency ω=2πf.

7.2 Simple harmonic motion

Simple harmonic motion (SHM) occurs when the restoring acceleration is directly proportional to displacement from equilibrium and directed towards equilibrium.

a = −ω²x;   x(t)=A cos(ωt+φ)

A is amplitude and φ is phase constant. Velocity and acceleration are:

v = −Aω sin(ωt+φ);   a = −Aω² cos(ωt+φ)

Maximum speed is Aω and maximum acceleration is Aω². At the mean position, speed is maximum and acceleration is zero. At extreme positions, speed is zero and acceleration magnitude is maximum.

7.3 Energy in SHM

For an ideal mass-spring oscillator, total mechanical energy is constant:

E = ½kA² = ½mω²A²

Potential energy is greatest at extreme positions; kinetic energy is greatest at equilibrium. In a real oscillator, damping gradually removes mechanical energy.

7.4 Torsional pendulum

A torsional pendulum consists of a body suspended by a thin wire. Twisting the body through an angle θ produces a restoring torque proportional to the angle for small twists.

τ = −Cθ;   T = 2π√(I/C)

C is couple per unit twist (torsional constant) and I is the moment of inertia of the suspended body about the wire axis. The period increases with I and decreases with C.

7.5 Couple per unit twist and rigidity modulus

For a uniform circular wire of length L and radius r, the torsional constant is:

C = πηr⁴/(2L)

Here η denotes the rigidity modulus of the wire (some books use G). Thus η=2LC/(πr⁴). The fourth power of radius makes accurate radius measurement especially important.

7.6 Moment of inertia of a disc

For a uniform solid disc of mass M and radius R about its central axis perpendicular to its plane:

I = ½MR²

For an experimental torsional pendulum, the moment of inertia can be determined from period and torsional constant, I=CT²/(4π²). The disc's geometric value can be compared with the experimental result.

8. Waves on a stretched string, energy and power

8.1 Wave basics

A wave transfers energy from one location to another without requiring net transport of the medium over a complete cycle. A transverse wave has particle motion perpendicular to the direction of propagation; a longitudinal wave has particle motion parallel to propagation.

v = fλ = ω/k

v is wave speed, f frequency, λ wavelength, ω angular frequency and k=2π/λ wave number.

8.2 Wave speed on a stretched string

v = √(T/μ)

T is tension and μ is mass per unit length. Increasing tension increases wave speed; increasing linear density decreases it.

8.3 Standing waves

When two waves of the same frequency and amplitude travel in opposite directions, they can superpose to form a standing wave. Nodes are points of zero displacement; antinodes are points of maximum displacement. For a string fixed at both ends:

fₙ = n v/(2L), n=1,2,3,...

The fundamental frequency is v/(2L); higher modes are harmonics. The allowed wavelengths are λₙ=2L/n.

8.4 Energy and power

A mechanical wave carries energy. For a sinusoidal transverse wave on a string, average power depends on the square of amplitude and angular frequency, and on the string's tension and mass density. A common expression is:

P̄ = ½ μω²A²v

This is the average power carried in one direction by a sinusoidal travelling wave on an ideal string. Do not confuse average power with instantaneous power.

9. Ultrasonics and generation methods

Ultrasonic waves are sound waves with frequencies above the upper limit of typical human hearing (about 20 kHz). Their short wavelengths at high frequency allow directional beams and fine-scale detection.

9.1 Piezoelectric effect

Certain crystals develop electric charge when mechanically stressed (direct piezoelectric effect). Conversely, applying an alternating electric field causes mechanical deformation or vibration (inverse piezoelectric effect). The inverse effect is used to generate ultrasonic waves.

9.2 Piezoelectric generator

  1. A piezoelectric crystal is placed between electrodes.
  2. An alternating voltage is applied.
  3. The crystal expands and contracts due to the inverse piezoelectric effect.
  4. At resonance, vibration amplitude becomes large and ultrasonic waves are emitted into the coupled medium.

Frequency depends on the crystal's dimensions, elastic properties and vibration mode. Good acoustic coupling is required to transfer energy efficiently.

9.3 Applications of ultrasonics

  • Non-destructive testing to detect internal flaws in metals and components.
  • Medical imaging and therapeutic applications.
  • Ultrasonic cleaning of precision parts.
  • Distance measurement and level sensing.
  • Welding, machining and process applications.

9.4 Acoustic grating

An acoustic grating is a periodic variation in refractive index produced by ultrasonic waves in a transparent liquid. The periodic density variation acts like a diffraction grating for light. By observing diffraction, the wavelength of the ultrasonic wave can be estimated. The exact working equation depends on the optical geometry and order used; use the laboratory's specified arrangement and formula.

10. Electromagnetic waves

10.1 Maxwell's equations — physical meaning

Electromagnetic fields are described by Maxwell's equations. In differential form (SI units):

Gauss law: ∇·E = ρ/ε₀
Gauss law for magnetism: ∇·B = 0
Faraday law: ∇×E = −∂B/∂t
Ampère–Maxwell law: ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t

Electric charges create electric fields; magnetic monopoles are not observed in classical electromagnetism; changing magnetic fields induce electric fields; currents and changing electric fields create magnetic fields.

10.2 Electromagnetic wave equation

In a source-free vacuum, Maxwell's equations lead to wave equations for electric and magnetic fields:

∇²E = μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t²;   ∇²B = μ₀ε₀ ∂²B/∂t²
c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3×10⁸ m s⁻¹

In a plane electromagnetic wave in free space, E and B are mutually perpendicular and both are perpendicular to the direction of propagation. They oscillate in phase and E=cB for field magnitudes.

10.3 Production by a dipole antenna

An alternating current in an antenna causes charges to accelerate and produces time-varying electric and magnetic fields. These fields propagate outward as electromagnetic radiation. Antenna dimensions and operating frequency determine radiation characteristics; a half-wave dipole has a characteristic length close to half the wavelength, subject to practical end effects.

10.4 Propagation and cell phone reception

Radio-frequency electromagnetic waves can propagate through free space and interact with buildings, terrain, atmosphere and materials. A mobile phone antenna receives a time-varying electromagnetic field and converts the induced electrical signal into a form that receiver circuitry can process. Reception depends on distance, frequency, antenna orientation, obstacles, multipath propagation, interference and network infrastructure.

Transmitting antenna))) EM wave (((Receiving antenna

The radio link carries modulated information; the receiver demodulates and processes it.

11. Quantum mechanics

11.1 Black-body radiation

A black body is an ideal object that absorbs all incident radiation and emits radiation with a spectrum determined by its temperature. Classical physics could not correctly explain the observed high-frequency spectrum (the ultraviolet catastrophe). Planck proposed that energy exchange occurs in discrete packets:

E = nhf, where n=0,1,2,...

h is Planck's constant and f is frequency. Quantisation successfully explained the black-body spectrum and introduced a central idea of quantum theory.

11.2 Photoelectric effect

When light of sufficiently high frequency falls on a suitable metal surface, electrons are emitted. The observations include a threshold frequency, emission with negligible delay under suitable conditions, and maximum electron kinetic energy depending on frequency rather than intensity.

Einstein equation: hf = φ + Kₘₐₓ = φ + eVₛ

φ is the work function, Kmax is maximum kinetic energy and Vs is stopping potential. Threshold frequency f₀=φ/h. Increasing intensity above threshold increases the number of emitted electrons (photocurrent), while increasing frequency increases their maximum kinetic energy.

Example: If incident photon energy is 5 eV and the work function is 2 eV, maximum photoelectron kinetic energy is 3 eV. The stopping potential magnitude is 3 V for an electron.

11.3 de Broglie hypothesis

Louis de Broglie proposed that particles have wave properties. The associated wavelength is:

λ = h/p = h/(mv) (non-relativistic case)

For an electron accelerated through a potential difference V, its kinetic energy is eV and, in the non-relativistic approximation, λ=h/√(2meV). At high energies, relativistic corrections are required.

11.4 Schrödinger wave equation

The wavefunction ψ describes the quantum state. The time-independent Schrödinger equation for a particle of mass m in potential energy V is:

−(ℏ²/2m)∇²ψ + Vψ = Eψ

In one dimension: −(ℏ²/2m)d²ψ/dx²+Vψ=Eψ. The wavefunction itself is not directly a probability; |ψ|² gives probability density after proper normalisation.

11.5 Particle in a one-dimensional infinite potential well

For a particle confined between x=0 and x=L with infinite potential walls, ψ must vanish at the walls. The allowed stationary states are:

ψₙ(x)=√(2/L) sin(nπx/L), n=1,2,3,...
Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²)

Energy is quantised. The ground-state energy is non-zero (zero-point energy). Higher states have energies proportional to n².

11.6 Three-dimensional box

For a rectangular box with side lengths Lx, Ly and Lz, the energy levels are:

E = (h²/8m)[nₓ²/Lₓ²+nᵧ²/Lᵧ²+n_z²/L_z²]

Each quantum number is a positive integer. Different combinations can sometimes produce the same energy, called degeneracy.

11.7 Barrier penetration and quantum tunnelling

Classically, a particle with energy below a potential barrier cannot cross it. Quantum mechanically, the wavefunction can extend into and through a finite barrier, giving a non-zero transmission probability. This is quantum tunnelling.

The probability decreases rapidly as barrier width and height increase, and depends on particle mass and energy. Tunnelling is important in scanning tunnelling microscopy, tunnel diodes, nuclear processes and nanoscale devices.

STM connection: In a scanning tunnelling microscope, a sharp conducting tip is brought extremely close to a conducting sample. A bias voltage allows tunnelling current to flow across the gap. The current changes strongly with tip-sample separation, enabling surface imaging at atomic scale.

12. Applied optics — interference

12.1 Superposition and interference

When two coherent light waves overlap, their electric fields superpose. Depending on phase difference, the resultant intensity can be larger (constructive interference) or smaller (destructive interference). Stable interference requires coherent sources with a constant phase relationship and suitable polarisation.

Constructive: path difference = mλ; destructive: path difference = (m+½)λ

m is an integer. Phase difference δ and path difference Δ are related by δ=2πΔ/λ.

12.2 Air wedge

An air wedge is a thin wedge-shaped air film formed between two glass plates. Monochromatic light reflected from the top and bottom surfaces of the film interferes, producing straight, parallel fringes. Because one reflected ray undergoes a phase reversal, the contact fringe is dark in reflected light for the usual arrangement.

For near-normal incidence, dark-fringe condition: 2t = mλ (with m=0 at contact)

For a small wedge angle θ, film thickness at distance x is t≈xθ. The fringe width is β=λ/(2θ). If the wedge length is l and spacer thickness is d, θ≈d/l and β≈λl/(2d).

12.3 Thickness of a thin sheet or wire using air wedge

Insert the sheet or wire at one end of the wedge and measure the fringe width β using a travelling microscope. If wedge length l and wavelength λ are known, thickness d=λl/(2β), under the small-angle geometry and reflected-light dark-fringe arrangement. Take readings across multiple fringes to reduce measurement error.

12.4 Michelson interferometer

A Michelson interferometer splits a light beam into two perpendicular paths using a partially reflecting plate. Mirrors reflect the beams back; they recombine and interfere. Moving one mirror by distance x changes the round-trip optical path by 2x, producing a fringe shift.

Number of fringe shifts N = 2x/λ; hence λ=2x/N

The instrument is used for precise wavelength measurement, small displacement measurement and studying changes in optical path length. Alignment, vibration isolation and a stable source are important.

13. Lasers — Einstein coefficients and laser systems

13.1 Spontaneous and stimulated emission

An atom or molecule in an excited energy state can return to a lower state and emit a photon. Spontaneous emission occurs without an external photon triggering the transition. Stimulated emission occurs when an incident photon induces emission of a second photon with matching frequency, phase, direction and polarisation.

Absorption raises a system to a higher energy state. For a transition between E₂ and E₁:

hν = E₂ − E₁

13.2 Einstein coefficients

  • A₂₁: probability rate coefficient for spontaneous emission.
  • B₂₁: coefficient for stimulated emission due to radiation energy density.
  • B₁₂: coefficient for absorption.

At thermal equilibrium, Einstein's relations connect the coefficients. For non-degenerate levels, B₁₂=B₂₁; in the general case, degeneracy factors must be included. A₂₁/B₂₁=8πhν³/c³ for the usual spectral energy-density convention.

13.3 Population inversion and optical resonator

Laser action requires population inversion: more particles occupy the upper laser level than the lower level for the transition of interest (with degeneracy taken into account). Pumping supplies energy. An optical resonator with mirrors provides feedback; one mirror is partially transmitting to allow output.

Pump source→Active medium between mirrors→Laser output

The active medium amplifies light by stimulated emission; the resonator provides optical feedback.

13.4 Properties of laser light

  • High directionality and low beam divergence.
  • High spectral coherence compared with ordinary light sources.
  • Often narrow spectral bandwidth.
  • Can be focused to high intensity.

13.5 Nd:YAG laser

Nd:YAG is a solid-state laser in which neodymium ions (Nd³⁺) are the active lasing centres in a yttrium aluminium garnet crystal host. It is commonly pumped by a flash lamp or laser diode. The principal infrared output wavelength is about 1.064 μm. Applications include material processing, marking, medical procedures and scientific instrumentation.

13.6 CO₂ laser

A CO₂ laser is a gas laser using a mixture typically containing carbon dioxide, nitrogen and helium. Electrical discharge excites nitrogen molecules; energy is transferred to CO₂ molecules, producing population inversion in vibrational energy levels. The laser commonly emits strongly at 10.6 μm (with another important band near 9.6 μm). It is widely used for cutting, engraving, welding and other material-processing operations.

13.7 Dye laser

A dye laser uses an organic dye solution as the active medium. Optical pumping excites dye molecules. A broad gain bandwidth allows wavelength tuning over a range using an optical resonator and tuning elements. Dye lasers are useful in spectroscopy and research, though liquid handling and dye degradation require care.

14. Optical fibre communication and fibre sensors

14.1 Structure of an optical fibre

An optical fibre consists of a high-refractive-index core surrounded by a lower-refractive-index cladding, with a protective coating or jacket. Light is guided along the core by total internal reflection (in a simple ray picture).

Protective jacketsurroundsCladding (lower n)surroundsCore (higher n)

14.2 Total internal reflection and numerical aperture

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from a higher refractive-index medium to a lower one and the incidence angle exceeds the critical angle. For core index n₁ and cladding index n₂, n₁>n₂:

sin θc = n₂/n₁

For a fibre in air, numerical aperture NA≈√(n₁²−n₂²) for the ideal step-index case. It indicates the light-acceptance capability. In an external medium of refractive index n₀, n₀sinθₐ=NA.

14.3 Fibre-optic communication system

Information source→Transmitter / LED or laser→Optical fibre link→Photodetector / receiver

The transmitter converts electrical information into modulated optical signals. The fibre guides light. The photodetector converts received optical power back into an electrical signal, which is amplified and decoded.

14.4 Fibre-optic sensors

A fibre sensor uses changes in light intensity, phase, wavelength or polarisation to detect a physical quantity. External conditions can alter the optical path or coupling.

SensorWhat changesExample application
DisplacementCoupling or reflected intensity varies with distance.Position measurement.
PressureDiaphragm deformation changes optical path or intensity.Pressure monitoring.
TemperatureRefractive index, length or spectral response changes with temperature.Remote temperature sensing.

Fibre sensors are useful in electrically noisy environments and remote or hard-to-reach locations. Sensor design must account for calibration, temperature cross-sensitivity, bending losses and connector losses.

15. Practical experiments and record preparation

The R-2025 syllabus explicitly lists the following practicals and activity demonstrations. Use your college laboratory manual for apparatus model, exact dimensions, observation-table headings and the prescribed calculation method.

15.1 Non-uniform bending — Young's modulus

Aim: determine Young's modulus of the beam material using non-uniform bending of a cantilever.

Principle: end-loaded cantilever deflection y=WL³/(3EI), with I=bd³/12 for a rectangular cross-section.

Record: beam length, breadth, thickness, load, depression, graph, slope and calculated modulus.

Precautions: rigid clamping, small elastic deflection, accurate thickness measurement, no parallax and steady readings.

15.2 Torsional pendulum — rigidity modulus and moment of inertia

Aim: determine rigidity modulus of the wire and moment of inertia of the disc using the torsional pendulum.

Principle: T=2π√(I/C), with C=πηr⁴/(2L) for a circular wire. Determine the period from repeated oscillations rather than timing one cycle.

Record: wire length and radius, disc mass and dimensions, time for a fixed number of oscillations, period, torsional constant and calculated values.

Precautions: small angular twist, avoid lateral swinging, measure wire radius accurately, and time many oscillations.

15.3 Melde's string experiment

Aim: determine the frequency of an electrically vibrating metal tip using a stretched string.

Principle: standing waves form when the string's driving frequency matches a natural mode. For a string fixed at both ends, fₙ=nv/(2L) and v=√(T/μ). The precise relation between vibrator frequency and string frequency depends on whether the arrangement is transverse or longitudinal; identify the apparatus configuration before applying the formula.

Record: vibrating length, tension, mass per unit length, number of loops, frequency and calculated result.

15.4 Photoelectric effect — Planck's constant

Aim: determine Planck's constant using photoelectric measurements.

Principle: eVₛ=hf−φ. Plot stopping potential Vs against frequency f; slope = h/e, so h=e×slope.

Record: wavelength or frequency, stopping potential, graph, slope and calculated h. Convert wavelength to frequency using f=c/λ.

Precautions: use the specified filters/source, avoid stray light, allow readings to stabilise, and maintain consistent stopping-potential determination.

15.5 Ruling width of a compact disc using laser

Aim: determine the track spacing/ruling width of a CD using laser diffraction.

Principle: the closely spaced tracks act as a reflection diffraction grating. For normal incidence, d sinθ=mλ. If screen distance is L and first-order spot displacement is x, θ=tan⁻¹(x/L); for small angles sinθ≈x/L.

Record: laser wavelength, screen distance, diffraction spot positions, order, angle and calculated spacing.

Precautions: never look directly into the laser beam; keep the beam at safe height, avoid reflective eye-level paths and use a screen to locate spots.

15.6 Thickness of thin sheet/wire using air wedge

Aim: determine the thickness of a thin object using interference fringes in an air wedge.

Principle: for a small wedge, β=λ/(2θ) and θ≈d/l, so thickness d=λl/(2β) for the standard reflected-light arrangement.

Record: wavelength, wedge length, fringe positions, average fringe width and calculated thickness.

16. Exam preparation and numerical-solving strategy

16.1 Formula selection

Before substituting values, identify the physical model. A cantilever uses beam deflection; a falling sphere uses Stokes' drag at terminal speed; a bimetallic strip uses differential thermal expansion; a torsional pendulum uses restoring torque; a standing string uses wave speed and boundary conditions; photoelectric stopping potential uses Einstein's equation; an air wedge uses interference geometry.

16.2 Unit conversion checklist

  • Convert mm to m before using SI formulae.
  • Convert grams to kilograms and millilitres to cubic metres where required.
  • Use temperature difference in kelvin (numerically equal to °C difference).
  • Use wavelength in metres and frequency in hertz.
  • Check whether a formula requires radius or diameter.
  • Write the final unit and check dimensional consistency.

16.3 Common mistakes

  • Confusing stiffness (Young's modulus) with strength or hardness.
  • Using beam thickness d as a linear rather than cubic quantity in cantilever deflection.
  • Applying Stokes' law when the flow is not in its low-Reynolds-number regime.
  • Forgetting that terminal velocity occurs after acceleration has settled.
  • Using degrees rather than radians in SHM and wave equations where angular variables are in radians.
  • Forgetting the phase reversal in reflected thin-film interference.
  • Confusing light intensity with photon energy in the photoelectric effect.
  • Omitting Jacobian factors in coordinate changes (where relevant in optics/other maths).
