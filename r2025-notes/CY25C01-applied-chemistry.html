TOPIC 1

Water Technology

1.1 Why water quality matters in engineering

Water used in a home, municipal supply, boiler, cooling tower or manufacturing plant must satisfy the requirements of that application. Impurities can cause health problems, scaling, corrosion, deposits, poor heat transfer and process contamination. Therefore, water is analysed and treated before use.

1.2 Important water-quality parameters

ParameterMeaningEngineering significance
pHMeasure related to acidity or alkalinity.Affects corrosion, treatment chemistry and process suitability.
TurbidityCloudiness caused by suspended particles.Indicates the need for clarification and filtration.
Total dissolved solids (TDS)Amount of dissolved inorganic and organic substances.Important in boiler feed, cooling and drinking-water treatment.
HardnessMainly due to dissolved calcium and magnesium ions.Causes scale and reduces soap efficiency.
AlkalinityCapacity to neutralise added acid, mainly from bicarbonates, carbonates and hydroxides.Influences buffering and treatment chemical demand.
Dissolved oxygen (DO)Oxygen dissolved in water.Can contribute to corrosion; also indicates water quality in environmental analysis.
ChloridesDissolved chloride ions.Can increase corrosion risk and affect suitability for use.

1.3 Hardness of water

Hard water contains appreciable amounts of calcium and magnesium salts. It consumes more soap and may form deposits when heated. Hardness is commonly expressed as mg/L (or ppm) as calcium carbonate equivalent.

Temporary hardnessMainly associated with bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium. It can be reduced by boiling or suitable lime treatment.
Permanent hardnessAssociated with chlorides, sulphates and other non-bicarbonate salts of calcium and magnesium. It is not removed by ordinary boiling.

Problems caused by hard water

  • Scale deposits on boiler and heat-exchanger surfaces.
  • Reduced heat transfer and increased fuel consumption.
  • Choking of pipes and reduced flow area.
  • Increased soap consumption and formation of scum.

1.4 Municipal water treatment

A conventional treatment plant removes suspended matter, reduces microbial risk and adjusts water quality for distribution. The exact process depends on raw-water quality and local standards.

  1. Screening: removes large floating matter such as leaves and debris.
  2. Aeration (where required): brings water into contact with air to help remove certain dissolved gases and oxidise selected impurities.
  3. Coagulation: a coagulant such as alum destabilises fine colloidal particles.
  4. Flocculation: gentle mixing encourages small particles to form larger flocs.
  5. Sedimentation: flocs settle under gravity and are removed as sludge.
  6. Filtration: water passes through filter media to remove remaining suspended particles.
  7. Disinfection: a suitable method is used to inactivate disease-causing microorganisms.
  8. Storage and distribution: treated water is stored and supplied while maintaining quality.
Alum coagulation: Alum helps fine colloidal particles combine into larger flocs that can settle and be filtered. It is not a substitute for the complete treatment and disinfection process.

1.5 Industrial feed-water problems

ProblemWhat happensWhy it matters
ScaleHard, adherent deposits form on hot surfaces.Poor heat transfer, overheating and reduced efficiency.
SludgeSoft, loose deposits accumulate in cooler or low-flow regions.Blocks passages and requires blowdown/cleaning.
PrimingWater droplets are carried with steam.Wet steam and contamination of downstream equipment.
FoamingPersistent bubbles form on the boiler-water surface.Promotes carryover of water with steam.
Boiler corrosionMetal is attacked by dissolved gases or unsuitable water chemistry.Wall thinning, leakage and failure risk.

1.6 Water remediation and desalination

Remediation means improving water quality by removing or reducing contaminants. Treatment may include filtration, adsorption, ion exchange, membrane processes, oxidation and disinfection, selected according to the contaminant.

Desalination removes dissolved salts from saline or brackish water. Major approaches include reverse osmosis (pressure-driven membrane separation) and thermal processes (evaporation followed by condensation).

Exam answer plan: municipal water treatment

Begin with the purpose of treatment. Draw a simple flow diagram, then explain screening, coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration and disinfection in order. Mention sludge removal and the need for treated-water storage. Label the flow diagram clearly.

TOPIC 2

Nano-chemistry

2.1 What are nanomaterials?

Nanomaterials have at least one characteristic dimension in the nanoscale range, commonly discussed as approximately 1–100 nm. At this scale, surface effects and size-dependent behaviour can make their properties different from those of the same material in bulk form.

2.2 Classification by dimensionality

ClassConfinement descriptionExamples
0DAll three dimensions are nanoscale.Nanoparticles, quantum dots.
1DTwo dimensions are nanoscale; one dimension is extended.Nanowires, nanotubes, nanorods.
2DOne dimension is nanoscale; two dimensions are extended.Thin nanosheets, graphene-like sheets.
3D nanostructuredBulk structure contains nanoscale features.Nanocrystalline solids, nanoporous materials.

2.3 Why properties change with size

  • High surface-area-to-volume ratio: a larger fraction of atoms lies at or near the surface, increasing the importance of surface reactions.
  • Optical effects: light absorption and emission may depend on particle size and electronic structure.
  • Electrical behaviour: charge transport can change with dimensions, interfaces and quantum confinement.
  • Mechanical behaviour: strength and deformation may change with grain size and defect structure.
  • Catalytic activity: increased accessible surface area can improve reaction activity for suitable materials.

2.4 Top-down and bottom-up preparation

Top-down approachStarts with bulk material and reduces it to nanoscale structures by methods such as milling, lithography or etching. Possible limitations include defects, contamination and material wastage.
Bottom-up approachBuilds nanostructures from atoms, ions or molecules using chemical or physical processes such as sol-gel synthesis, precipitation or self-assembly. Control of growth and agglomeration is important.

2.5 Sol-gel method

The sol-gel method is a bottom-up wet-chemical route used to prepare oxide nanomaterials and coatings. A precursor solution undergoes hydrolysis and condensation to form a colloidal sol, which develops into a gel network. Drying and heat treatment produce the desired solid material.

  1. Precursor preparation: dissolve a suitable metal alkoxide or salt in a controlled solvent system.
  2. Hydrolysis: water reacts with the precursor to form reactive hydroxyl groups.
  3. Condensation: the reactive species link together to form a network.
  4. Sol formation: small particles or polymeric species remain dispersed in the liquid.
  5. Gelation and ageing: a connected network develops and strengthens.
  6. Drying: liquid is removed to form a dried gel.
  7. Calcination: controlled heating removes residual organics and develops the required oxide structure.
Safety: Nanopowders may be inhalation hazards and some precursors/solvents are hazardous. Laboratory preparation must follow the approved risk assessment, PPE and waste-disposal procedure.
TOPIC 3

Electrochemistry

3.1 Redox reactions

Electrochemistry studies the relationship between chemical reactions and electrical energy. A redox reaction involves electron transfer: oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain of electrons.

OxidationElectrons are released. Example: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
ReductionElectrons are accepted. Example: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

3.2 Electrochemical cell

An electrochemical cell uses redox reactions to convert chemical energy into electrical energy or, in an electrolytic cell, uses electrical energy to drive a chemical reaction.

In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction occurs at the cathode. Electrons travel through the external circuit from anode to cathode. Ions move through the electrolyte and salt bridge/membrane to maintain charge balance.

Daniell cell — illustrative example

Cell notation: Zn | Zn²⁺ || Cu²⁺ | Cu

Anode reaction: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻

Cathode reaction: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

Overall reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

Zinc is oxidised and copper ions are reduced. The electrons move through the external wire from the zinc electrode to the copper electrode.

3.3 Electrode potential and cell EMF

Electrode potential describes the tendency of an electrode reaction to occur in the reduction direction under specified conditions, measured relative to a reference electrode. Standard electrode potentials are tabulated as reduction potentials.

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

For a galvanic cell under standard conditions, a positive standard cell potential indicates a thermodynamically feasible spontaneous cell reaction as written. Actual cell potential depends on concentration, pressure and temperature.

3.4 Nernst equation

The Nernst equation relates electrode or cell potential to the reaction quotient under non-standard conditions.

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

At 25 °C, for base-10 logarithms:

E = E° − (0.0591/n) log₁₀ Q

Here, n is the number of electrons transferred, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, F is Faraday's constant and Q is the reaction quotient.

3.5 Conductivity of electrolytes

Electrolytic conduction occurs due to the movement of ions in solution or molten state. Conductivity depends on the number of ions, their charge and mobility, temperature, solvent properties and concentration.

QuantityMeaningUnit
Resistance (R)Opposition to electric current.ohm (Ω)
Conductance (G)Reciprocal of resistance.siemens (S)
Conductivity (κ)Conductance corrected for cell geometry.S m⁻¹ or S cm⁻¹
Molar conductivity (Λm)Conductivity contribution per mole of electrolyte.Commonly S cm² mol⁻¹
G = 1/R
κ = G × (l/A)

Here, l is the distance between electrodes and A is the effective electrode area. The ratio l/A is the cell constant.

3.6 Conductometric titration

Conductometric titration follows the change in electrical conductivity as titrant is added. The conductivity changes because ions are consumed, produced or replaced by ions with different mobilities. The end point is obtained from the intersection of the appropriate straight-line portions of a conductivity-versus-volume plot.

  1. Rinse the conductivity cell and place it in the analyte solution.
  2. Record the initial conductivity.
  3. Add titrant in measured increments and mix consistently.
  4. Record conductivity after each addition.
  5. Plot conductivity against titrant volume.
  6. Locate the end point from the change in slope/intersection.
TOPIC 4

Corrosion and Control

4.1 Definition and importance

Corrosion is the deterioration of a material, usually a metal, due to interaction with its environment. It can reduce strength, cause leakage, contaminate products, increase maintenance cost and lead to sudden equipment failure.

4.2 Chemical (dry) corrosion

Dry corrosion occurs by direct chemical reaction between a metal and gases such as oxygen, usually without an aqueous electrolyte. An oxide or other reaction product forms on the metal surface. The protectiveness of the film depends on its adherence, porosity, stability and ability to prevent further diffusion.

4.3 Electrochemical (wet) corrosion

Wet corrosion occurs when anodic and cathodic reactions take place on a metal surface in the presence of an electrolyte. At the anode, metal atoms lose electrons and enter solution as ions. At the cathode, a reduction reaction consumes electrons.

Anodic reactionM → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻
Metal dissolution occurs at anodic regions.
Cathodic reactionO₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (neutral/alkaline aerated medium)
Another possible reaction is hydrogen-ion reduction in acidic medium.

4.4 Galvanic corrosion and galvanic series

When two dissimilar metals are electrically connected in an electrolyte, the more active metal generally becomes the anode and corrodes faster, while the more noble metal becomes the cathode. The galvanic series ranks materials by their relative corrosion potential in a specified environment.

Galvanic corrosion becomes more severe when the anodic area is small compared with the cathodic area, when the electrolyte is highly conductive, or when the metals have a large potential difference.

4.5 Factors influencing corrosion

  • Nature and composition of the metal and its surface condition.
  • Electrode-potential difference between coupled materials.
  • Presence of moisture, oxygen, salts and pollutants.
  • Temperature, pH and conductivity of the environment.
  • Relative anodic and cathodic areas.
  • Velocity of fluid and deposits that create differential aeration cells.
  • Mechanical stress and damage to protective films.

4.6 Corrosion-control methods

MethodPrincipleExamples / use
Material selectionSelect a material compatible with the environment.Corrosion-resistant alloys, suitable polymers or linings.
Good designAvoid crevices, stagnant zones and unfavourable metal-area ratios.Drainage, accessible joints, insulation between dissimilar metals.
Protective coatingSeparate metal from the environment or provide sacrificial protection.Paint, polymer coating, metallic coating.
Cathodic protectionMake the structure the cathode of an electrochemical system.Sacrificial anode or impressed-current method.
Anodic protectionMaintain selected metals in a passive state by controlled potential.Specialised chemical storage applications.
InhibitorsReduce corrosion reaction rate when added in controlled amounts.Closed cooling systems and process equipment.

4.7 Organic and inorganic coatings

Organic coatings include paints, varnishes, polymers and resins. They form a barrier between the metal and its environment. Surface preparation, coating thickness, adhesion and damage resistance are important.

Inorganic coatings include conversion coatings, ceramic or glass-like coatings and certain metallic/inorganic surface layers. They may provide chemical stability, wear resistance or a barrier against the environment.

Answer structure for corrosion protection: State the corrosion mechanism first, identify the environment and failure risk, then explain the selected control method with a labelled sketch or practical example. Do not simply list methods without stating how each one works.
TOPIC 5

Batteries and Energy Storage

5.1 What is a battery?

A battery is an electrochemical energy-storage device made from one or more cells. It converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy during discharge. Rechargeable batteries use an external electrical source to drive the reverse reaction during charging.

5.2 Primary and secondary batteries

Primary batteryDesigned mainly for one-time discharge; the cell reaction is not intended to be reversed by ordinary charging. Examples include common alkaline and primary lithium cells.
Secondary batteryDesigned to be recharged and used for multiple cycles. Examples include lead-acid, nickel-metal hydride and lithium-ion batteries.

5.3 Battery construction

A typical cell contains an anode, cathode, electrolyte, separator and current collectors. The separator prevents direct electrical contact between electrodes while allowing ionic transport. Current collectors connect the electrode materials to the external circuit.

5.4 Lithium-ion battery — working overview

In a typical lithium-ion cell, lithium ions move between the two electrode materials through the electrolyte during charge and discharge. Electrons move through the external circuit. The exact electrode reactions depend on the selected cell chemistry; therefore, do not assume every lithium-ion battery uses the same cathode composition.

  1. Discharge: lithium ions move internally from the negative electrode towards the positive electrode; electrons flow through the external load.
  2. Charge: an external charger drives the reverse direction of ion and electron movement.
  3. Control: the battery-management system monitors voltage, current and temperature and protects the pack against unsafe operating conditions.

5.5 Battery pack and battery-management system

A battery pack combines multiple cells in series and/or parallel to obtain the required voltage and capacity. A battery-management system (BMS) monitors cell or module conditions, estimates state of charge and state of health, balances cells where supported, and provides protection against over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current and excessive temperature.

5.6 Battery performance parameters

ParameterMeaningWhy it matters
EMF / nominal voltageElectrical potential associated with the cell under stated conditions.Determines voltage compatibility and pack configuration.
CapacityCharge deliverable under specified conditions, often in Ah.Indicates how much charge can be supplied.
EnergyElectrical work available, commonly Wh or kWh.Indicates stored energy for a load.
Internal resistanceEffective opposition inside the cell.Causes voltage drop and heat during current flow.
Power capabilityRate at which energy can be delivered.Important for acceleration, peak loads and power tools.
Cycle lifeNumber of charge-discharge cycles before a defined capacity limit is reached.Indicates expected service life.
Energy densityEnergy stored per unit mass or volume.Important for portable devices and electric vehicles.
Energy (Wh) ≈ nominal voltage (V) × capacity (Ah)
P = V × I

These are practical approximations for basic calculations; actual usable energy depends on discharge rate, temperature, operating limits and cell chemistry.

5.7 Battery testing and safety

Common tests include measuring open-circuit voltage/EMF, internal resistance, charge-discharge characteristics, capacity and temperature response. Testing must use a suitable instrument and follow the manufacturer's limits.

  • Do not short-circuit battery terminals.
  • Use the specified charger and charging limits.
  • Do not use swollen, damaged or overheated cells.
  • Protect packs from mechanical damage, water ingress and incorrect polarity.
  • Provide appropriate thermal management and electrical protection.
  • Handle and dispose of damaged cells through approved procedures.

EXAM ANSWER METHOD

How to write a strong chemistry answer

  1. Begin with a definition. Define the process or parameter in one or two precise sentences.
  2. Draw a labelled diagram. Use a treatment flow diagram, cell diagram, corrosion cell, sol-gel flow or battery structure where relevant.
  3. Explain the mechanism in sequence. Use numbered stages and balanced equations when the reaction is known.
  4. Connect to engineering use. State why the process matters in boilers, pipelines, electronics, manufacturing or electric vehicles.
  5. End with advantages, limitations or precautions. Include only points relevant to the question.
Common mistakes: confusing hardness with alkalinity; treating all corrosion as rusting; reversing oxidation and reduction; mixing up anode/cathode signs across galvanic and electrolytic cells; and confusing battery capacity (Ah) with energy (Wh).
