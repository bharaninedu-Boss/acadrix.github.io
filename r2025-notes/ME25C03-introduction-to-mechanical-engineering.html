COURSE OVERVIEW

What does mechanical engineering deal with?

Mechanical engineering applies mechanics, materials, energy and manufacturing knowledge to create, analyse, operate and improve machines and mechanical systems. It ranges from a small fastener to an engine, a refrigeration plant, a production line or a complete vehicle.

Central engineering sequence: identify a need → define requirements → analyse forces, materials and energy → select components and manufacturing methods → build and test → improve for safety, quality, cost and sustainability.

How to study this course

The R-2025 course is a foundation-level introduction. It does not replace later detailed courses such as Strength of Materials, Thermodynamics, Fluid Mechanics or Manufacturing Technology. Learn the physical meaning of each concept and practise simple calculations with units.

Syllabus scope: The five content blocks are Engineering; Mechanics of Materials and Structures; Energy Interactions; Machine Elements; and Manufacturing Processes. The activities listed in the curriculum are demonstrations, quizzes, unit conversions, software-based simple structural problems, working models and 3D printing.

1. Engineering foundations

1.1 History and evolution of mechanical engineering

Mechanical engineering developed from practical skills in making tools, lifting devices, mills, pumps, vehicles and machines. The Industrial Revolution accelerated the use of steam power, machine tools and factory production. Later developments included internal-combustion engines, electrical machines, aircraft, automation, computer-aided design and digital manufacturing.

Modern mechanical engineering combines traditional engineering science with electronics, computation, materials science and data. Examples include electric vehicles, industrial robots, additive manufacturing, energy-efficient buildings and predictive maintenance.

1.3 Force, motion and Newton's laws

Newton's first law: a body remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless acted upon by a non-zero resultant external force. It introduces inertia.

Newton's second law: the resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum. For constant mass:

ΣF = m a

where ΣF is resultant force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in m/s².

Newton's third law: forces between two interacting bodies are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction and act on different bodies. For example, a tyre pushes the road backward and the road exerts a forward friction force on the tyre.

1.4 Work, energy and power

For a constant force F acting through displacement s at angle θ to the displacement:

W = F s cos θ

For a body of mass m moving at speed v, kinetic energy is:

KE = ½ m v²

Near Earth's surface, gravitational potential energy relative to a chosen reference level is:

PE = m g h

Average power is work divided by time:

P = W/t

For steady rotation, mechanical power is the product of torque and angular speed:

P = T ω

where T is torque in N·m and ω is angular velocity in rad/s.

Worked example: A 20 kg component is lifted vertically through 3 m in 5 s. Taking g = 9.81 m/s², work done against gravity = mgh = 20 × 9.81 × 3 = 588.6 J. Average power = 588.6/5 = 117.72 W. This is ideal lifting power; actual input power is higher because of losses.

Dimensions describe the physical nature of a quantity in terms of base quantities. In basic mechanics, mass [M], length [L] and time [T] are commonly used.

1.8 Engineering ethics and professionalism

Engineering decisions affect people, property and the environment. Professional conduct includes protecting public safety, working within one's competence, reporting data honestly, respecting standards, avoiding conflicts of interest and taking responsibility for decisions.

  • Safety: identify hazards and reduce risk before operating or releasing a product.
  • Honesty: do not manipulate test results or hide a known defect.
  • Competence: use qualified review where a problem is beyond one's knowledge.
  • Confidentiality: protect legitimate client and employer information.
  • Sustainability: consider energy, materials, waste, repairability and product life.
  • Professional communication: document assumptions, limitations and decisions clearly.

2. Mechanics of Materials and Structures

2.1 External load and internal resistance

When a mechanical component is loaded, internal forces develop to resist the external action. The intensity of internal force over an area is described using stress. Deformation is measured using strain. These quantities help engineers decide whether a component is sufficiently strong and stiff for its purpose.

2.2 Normal stress

For a uniform axial load acting centrally on a prismatic bar, the average normal stress is:

σ = P/A

where σ is normal stress (Pa or N/mm²), P is axial load (N), and A is cross-sectional area (m² or mm²). Tensile stress tends to elongate a member; compressive stress tends to shorten it.

2.3 Shear stress

Shear stress acts parallel to the area under consideration and tends to make adjacent material layers slide relative to one another. For a simple average-stress estimate:

τ = V/A

where V is the resultant shear force. Actual shear-stress distribution depends on geometry and loading; the average formula is not a substitute for detailed analysis in complex sections.

2.4 Strain

Strain is deformation per unit original dimension. It is dimensionless.

ε = ΔL/L

where ΔL is change in length and L is original length. Tensile strain is positive under the usual sign convention; compressive strain is negative.

2.5 Elasticity and plasticity

Elastic deformation is recoverable when the load is removed, provided the material remains within its elastic range. Plastic deformation is permanent deformation that remains after unloading. A component should normally be designed to avoid unacceptable permanent deformation under its specified service loads.

PropertyMeaningEngineering importance
StrengthAbility to withstand stress without failure.Prevents yielding or fracture under design loads.
StiffnessResistance to elastic deformation.Controls deflection and alignment.
ToughnessAbility to absorb energy before fracture.Useful under impact or fluctuating loads.
HardnessResistance to indentation, scratching or wear.Important for contact surfaces and tools.
DuctilityAbility to undergo plastic deformation in tension.Allows forming and gives deformation before fracture.
BrittlenessTendency to fracture with little plastic deformation.Requires care where impact or defects occur.

2.6 Hooke's law and Young's modulus

For many materials under small uniaxial elastic deformation, stress is proportional to strain:

σ = E ε   ⇒   E = σ/ε

E is Young's modulus (Pa). A high Young's modulus indicates high elastic stiffness, not necessarily high strength.

2.7 Axial deformation of a uniform bar

Combining σ = P/A and ε = δ/L with Hooke's law gives, for a uniform prismatic bar under a constant axial load in the linear-elastic range:

δ = P L/(A E)

where δ is axial extension or shortening, L is length, A is area and E is Young's modulus. For stepped bars or varying loads, analyse each segment and add compatible deformations.

Worked example: A steel tie of length 2 m and area 200 mm² carries a tensile load of 20 kN. Take E = 200 GPa. Stress = 20,000/200 = 100 N/mm². Strain = 100/200,000 = 0.0005. Extension = 0.0005 × 2000 = 1.0 mm.

2.8 Introduction to bending

A beam is a structural member that carries loads mainly transverse to its longitudinal axis. Such loads produce shear force and bending moment. Bending causes one side of the beam to shorten (compression) and the opposite side to elongate (tension), with a neutral surface between them for simple elastic bending.

  • Simply supported beam: supported at two locations, commonly by a pin and roller idealisation.
  • Cantilever: fixed at one end and free at the other.
  • Overhanging beam: extends beyond one or both supports.

For a simply supported beam with a central point load W and span L, the support reactions are W/2 each by symmetry. Maximum bending moment at the centre is WL/4. This is a specific case, not a universal beam formula.

2.9 Introduction to torsion

Torsion is twisting caused by a torque about a member's longitudinal axis. Shafts transmit torque in motors, gearboxes and machines. Under torsion, shear stresses develop across the cross-section and the shaft rotates through an angle.

For a solid circular shaft in elastic torsion, the standard relation is:

T/J = τ/r = G θ/L

T = applied torque, J = polar second moment of area, τ = shear stress at radius r, G = shear modulus, θ = angle of twist in radians and L = shaft length. Detailed shaft design is studied in later courses.

2.10 Simple structural analysis and design concepts

A basic structural analysis begins by isolating the member and drawing a free-body diagram. Show all external forces, support reactions, dimensions and applied moments. Apply equilibrium in a plane:

ΣFₓ = 0,   ΣFᵧ = 0,   ΣM = 0

Then determine internal actions and check whether the member's strength, stiffness and stability are adequate. A design must also consider connections, material variability, manufacturing tolerances, fatigue, corrosion, safety factors and relevant standards.

Free-body diagram habit: remove the supports from the isolated body and replace them with the appropriate reaction components. Do not draw an unknown support reaction and then silently omit it from equilibrium equations.

3. Energy Interactions

3.1 System and surroundings

A thermodynamic system is the quantity of matter or region selected for study. Everything outside it is the surroundings; the boundary separates them. A boundary may be real or imaginary, fixed or moving.

System typeMass crossing boundary?Energy crossing boundary?Example
Closed systemNoYes, possibleGas in a sealed piston-cylinder.
Open system / control volumeYesYes, possibleTurbine, compressor, pump.
Isolated systemNoNoIdealised perfectly isolated system.

3.2 Energy transfer: heat and work

Energy can cross a system boundary as heat or work. Heat transfer occurs because of a temperature difference. Work is energy transfer associated with an organised force-displacement, shaft rotation, electrical effect or other work interaction. Heat and work are boundary interactions, not properties stored in a system.

3.3 Modes of heat transfer

Conduction
Energy transfer through a material due to microscopic interactions, without bulk motion of the material as a whole. Example: a metal rod heated at one end.
Convection
Heat transfer between a surface and a moving fluid. Example: air flowing over a hot radiator.
Radiation
Energy transfer by electromagnetic waves; it does not require a material medium. Example: solar energy reaching Earth.

3.4 Basic heat-transfer relations

For one-dimensional steady conduction through a plane wall with constant properties:

Q̇ = k A (T₁ − T₂)/L

For convection between a surface and a fluid:

Q̇ = h A (Tₛ − T∞)

For ideal thermal radiation exchange with a large surrounding enclosure:

Q̇ = ε σSB A (Tₛ⁴ − Tsur⁴)

Here Q̇ is heat-transfer rate (W), k is thermal conductivity (W/m·K), A is area (m²), L is wall thickness (m), h is convection coefficient (W/m²·K), ε is emissivity, σSB is Stefan–Boltzmann constant and temperatures in the radiation equation must be in kelvin.

3.5 Heat engines

A heat engine receives energy from a high-temperature source, converts part of it into work and rejects the remaining energy to a lower-temperature sink. It operates in a cycle.

ηth = Wnet/QH = 1 − QL/QH

where QH is heat supplied, QL is heat rejected and Wnet is net work output over a cycle. Thermal efficiency is less than 100% for a cyclic heat engine operating between finite-temperature reservoirs.

3.6 Refrigeration and HVAC systems

A refrigerator uses work input to remove heat from a low-temperature space and reject it to a higher-temperature environment. An air conditioner performs the same basic refrigeration function to control indoor temperature and often humidity. HVAC means heating, ventilation and air conditioning.

COPR = QL/Wnet     COPHP = QH/Wnet

Coefficient of performance (COP) compares desired heating or cooling effect with work input. Unlike heat-engine efficiency, COP can be greater than one because it measures heat moved per unit work, not conversion of heat into work.

3.7 Working principle of a vapour-compression refrigerator

  1. Evaporator: low-pressure refrigerant absorbs heat from the refrigerated space and evaporates.
  2. Compressor: vapour pressure and temperature are raised by work input.
  3. Condenser: high-pressure refrigerant rejects heat to the surroundings and condenses.
  4. Expansion device: pressure is reduced before refrigerant returns to the evaporator.
Remember the energy direction: refrigerator removes heat from the cold space; condenser releases heat to the room/outside. A refrigerator does not “create cold”; it transfers heat.

3.8 HVAC system components

A basic HVAC system may include a heating or cooling coil, fan, filters, ducts, dampers, controls, ventilation air and condensate drainage. Good design considers thermal comfort, indoor air quality, humidity, energy consumption, noise and maintenance access.

4. Machine Elements

Machine elements are basic components used repeatedly in mechanical systems. The correct selection depends on load, speed, motion, space, lubrication, environment, manufacturing method, maintenance and cost.

4.1 Gears

Gears transmit rotary motion and torque through meshing teeth. They can change speed, torque and direction. For an ideal external gear pair without slip at the pitch point:

n₁/n₂ = T₂/T₁ = Z₂/Z₁

where n is rotational speed, T is torque and Z is number of teeth. Subscripts 1 and 2 refer to driver and driven gears. The direction of rotation is opposite for a simple external gear pair.

Gear typeTypical use / feature
Spur gearParallel shafts; straight teeth; simple and efficient.
Helical gearParallel or crossed shaft arrangements; smoother engagement, but axial thrust may occur.
Bevel gearIntersecting shafts, often at 90°.
Worm and worm wheelNon-parallel, non-intersecting shafts; large speed reduction possible.
Rack and pinionConverts rotary motion to linear motion or vice versa.

4.2 Bearings

Bearings support and guide moving parts while reducing friction and controlling relative motion. They may carry radial loads, axial (thrust) loads or a combination.

  • Rolling-contact bearings: balls or rollers separate moving rings; examples include deep-groove ball, cylindrical roller, tapered roller and thrust bearings.
  • Plain bearings: sliding contact occurs between a shaft and bearing surface, often separated by a lubricant film.

Bearing selection considers load magnitude and direction, speed, required life, stiffness, misalignment, temperature, contamination, lubrication and mounting.

4.3 Shafts

A shaft is a rotating machine member used to transmit torque and support components such as gears, pulleys, sprockets and couplings. Shafts may experience torsion, bending, axial load and fatigue. Common materials include carbon and alloy steels, selected according to required strength, toughness, wear resistance and manufacturability.

4.4 Fasteners

Fasteners join components. Bolts and nuts create detachable joints; screws may engage a threaded hole; studs have threads at both ends. Washers distribute bearing pressure, protect surfaces or help resist loosening depending on type. A joint must be designed for the applied loads, vibration, environment, assembly access and maintenance requirements.

4.5 Couplings

Couplings connect two shafts to transmit torque. Rigid couplings require accurate alignment; flexible couplings can accommodate limited misalignment and reduce transmission of shock or vibration. A coupling is not a substitute for correcting excessive shaft misalignment.

4.6 Selection of machine components

  1. Define the function and required motion.
  2. Determine loads, speed, duty cycle and operating environment.
  3. Choose a suitable component type and material.
  4. Check strength, stiffness, life and compatibility with mating parts.
  5. Consider lubrication, sealing, assembly and maintenance.
  6. Compare availability, standards, cost, reliability and safety.

4.7 Quality control and safety

Quality control verifies that components and assemblies meet specified requirements. Methods include dimensional inspection, visual inspection, material certificates, functional tests and non-destructive testing where appropriate. Safety includes guarding rotating parts, preventing entanglement, controlling stored energy, using correct lifting methods and following lockout/tagout procedures during maintenance.

Workshop safety: Never touch a rotating shaft, gear or belt; do not remove guards while equipment is operating. Isolate and verify the energy source before adjustment or maintenance.

5. Manufacturing Processes

Manufacturing converts raw material into useful products through shaping, material removal, joining, heat treatment, finishing, inspection and assembly. Process choice affects accuracy, surface finish, strength, production rate, cost and environmental impact.

5.1 Casting

Casting produces a component by pouring molten material into a mould cavity and allowing it to solidify. It is useful for complex shapes and components that may be difficult to machine from solid stock.

  1. Prepare a pattern representing the required casting shape, with suitable allowances.
  2. Prepare the mould cavity and cores if internal passages are required.
  3. Melt and prepare the metal.
  4. Pour metal through the gating system into the cavity.
  5. Allow solidification and cooling.
  6. Remove the casting, cut gates/risers, clean and inspect.

Important terms: pattern, mould, core, cope, drag, sprue, runner, gate, riser, draft allowance, shrinkage allowance and machining allowance.

5.2 Forming processes

Forming changes shape by plastic deformation without intentionally removing material. Examples include forging, rolling, extrusion and drawing.

ProcessBasic actionTypical product
ForgingCompressive force from hammer or press reshapes heated or cold metal.Crankshafts, connecting rods, tools.
RollingMaterial passes between rotating rolls to reduce thickness or change section.Sheets, plates, rails, structural sections.
ExtrusionBillet is forced through a die opening.Rods, tubes, aluminium profiles.
DrawingMaterial is pulled through a die to reduce cross-section.Wires, rods, tubes.

5.3 Machining

Machining removes material from a workpiece as chips to obtain the required shape, size and finish. Common processes include turning, drilling, milling, shaping, grinding and boring.

  • Turning: workpiece rotates while a cutting tool removes material, commonly on a lathe.
  • Drilling: a rotating drill produces a hole.
  • Milling: a rotating multi-edge cutter removes material as the workpiece or cutter is fed.
  • Grinding: an abrasive wheel removes small amounts of material for accuracy and finish.
  • Boring: enlarges or finishes an existing hole.

5.4 Joining processes

Joining combines two or more parts. Common categories include welding, brazing, soldering, mechanical fastening and adhesive bonding.

MethodPrincipleTypical distinction
WeldingCoalescence of materials, with or without filler and pressure.Often produces a permanent metallurgical joint.
BrazingFiller metal melts above 450°C (approximately) but base metals do not melt.Filler flows into a close-fitting joint by capillary action.
SolderingFiller metal melts below 450°C (approximately); base metals do not melt.Used for electrical and light-duty joints.
BoltingThreaded fasteners clamp components together.Detachable joint for maintenance.
Adhesive bondingAdhesive layer transfers load between prepared surfaces.Can join dissimilar materials; surface preparation is important.

5.5 CNC machining

Computer Numerical Control (CNC) uses a programmed controller to direct machine motions and auxiliary functions. A CNC system typically includes a part program, controller, drive system, machine tool, feedback devices where applicable and work/tool holding.

General workflow: create or obtain a drawing/model → plan operations → select tools and workholding → generate or write the program → simulate/check → set work and tool offsets → run safely → inspect the part. CNC improves repeatability and supports complex geometry, but programming, setup, tooling and inspection remain essential.

5.6 Additive manufacturing

Additive manufacturing creates a part by adding material, commonly layer by layer, from digital geometry. In polymer FDM/FFF, a thermoplastic filament is heated and deposited through a nozzle. Other processes include vat photopolymerisation, powder-bed fusion and directed energy deposition.

Advantages
Complex geometry, internal channels, rapid prototypes, low tooling for some parts and part consolidation.
Limitations
Build time, material/process limits, surface finish, anisotropy, support removal and post-processing.

5.7 Smart manufacturing

Smart manufacturing uses connected machines, sensors, automation, software and data to improve production. It may combine industrial IoT, robotics, machine vision, digital twins, analytics, cloud/edge computing and predictive maintenance.

A sensor can measure vibration or temperature; data can be analysed to identify abnormal operation; maintenance can then be planned before a failure causes long downtime. Data quality, cybersecurity, interoperability, workforce skills and safe human-machine interaction are important challenges.

5.8 Comparing process choices

RequirementProcess considerations

6. Worked calculations and practice

Example 1 — SI unit conversion

A vehicle travels at 72 km/h. Convert the speed into m/s.

  1. Use 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s.
  2. Speed = 72 × 5/18.
  3. Speed = 20 m/s.

Example 2 — Resultant force

A 12 kg trolley accelerates at 2.5 m/s² on a horizontal surface. Find the resultant force.

ΣF = ma = 12 × 2.5 = 30 N

The resultant force is 30 N in the direction of acceleration. If friction acts, the applied force must be greater than 30 N to produce this net force.

Example 3 — Average normal stress

A tie rod of cross-sectional area 150 mm² carries a tensile load of 15 kN.

σ = P/A = 15,000/150 = 100 N/mm² = 100 MPa

The average tensile stress is 100 MPa.

Example 4 — Gear speed ratio

A 20-tooth driving gear rotates at 900 rpm and drives a 60-tooth gear. Find the driven speed for an ideal external gear pair.

n₂ = n₁(Z₁/Z₂) = 900(20/60) = 300 rpm

The driven gear rotates at 300 rpm, in the opposite direction.

Example 5 — Power from torque and speed

A shaft transmits 40 N·m torque at 1500 rpm. Find the ideal mechanical power.

  1. Angular speed ω = 2πN/60 = 2π(1500)/60 = 157.08 rad/s.
  2. P = Tω = 40 × 157.08 = 6283 W.
  3. Power ≈ 6.28 kW.

Practice questions

  1. Convert 90 km/h into m/s.
  2. A 25 kg body accelerates at 1.8 m/s². Find the resultant force.
  3. A tensile load of 24 kN acts on an area of 300 mm². Find average normal stress.
  4. A bar 1.5 m long extends 0.6 mm. Find the normal strain.
  5. A 10 kg object is lifted by 2.5 m. Calculate the increase in gravitational potential energy using g = 9.81 m/s².
  6. A 24-tooth gear drives a 72-tooth gear at 600 rpm. Find the driven speed.
  7. State two differences between conduction and convection.
  8. Explain why the COP of a refrigerator can be greater than one.
  9. Differentiate casting and forging.
  10. List four factors affecting bearing selection.

Answer check

  1. 25 m/s.
  2. 45 N.
  3. 80 N/mm² (80 MPa).
  4. 0.0004.
  5. 245.25 J.
  6. 200 rpm, opposite direction for a simple external gear pair.
  7. Conduction is transfer through material interactions; convection involves fluid motion and surface-fluid interaction.
  8. COP is heat removed divided by work input, not an energy-conversion efficiency.
  9. Casting solidifies molten material in a mould; forging shapes solid material by compressive deformation.
  10. Load type/magnitude, speed, required life, lubrication, temperature, contamination, alignment and space.

7. Syllabus activities and demonstrations

7.1 Interactive quiz

Prepare a short quiz covering force, motion, energy, units, stress, strain, heat transfer, gears, bearings and manufacturing. For every answer, explain why the selected option is correct.

7.2 Unit conversion activity

  1. Select a physical quantity and write its original value and unit.
  2. Write the conversion relationship.
  3. Cancel units systematically.
  4. Check whether the resulting unit matches the required quantity.
  5. Estimate whether the magnitude is reasonable.

7.3 Simple truss or beam analysis using software

The curriculum calls for a demonstration of simple truss or beam problems solved using software. A safe learning workflow is:

  1. Draw the geometry and identify joints, members, supports and loads.
  2. State assumptions such as ideal pin joints, static loading and member behaviour.
  3. Calculate support reactions by equilibrium before or alongside the software model.
  4. Enter geometry, material properties, supports and loads carefully.
  5. Run the analysis and inspect reactions, member forces or deflection.
  6. Compare the software result with hand calculations and explain differences.

Software output is only as reliable as the model, boundary conditions, units and assumptions entered.

7.4 Working-model demonstrations

For an internal-combustion engine model, identify cylinder, piston, connecting rod, crankshaft, inlet/exhaust valves and spark plug or injector as appropriate to the engine type. Explain how reciprocating piston motion is converted into crankshaft rotation.

For a refrigerator model, identify compressor, condenser, expansion device and evaporator. Trace the refrigerant flow and indicate where heat enters and leaves the cycle.

7.5 Virtual thermodynamic cycles

Observe how pressure, volume and temperature change during a cycle. Identify compression, heat addition, expansion and heat rejection where applicable. Do not assume all engines or refrigeration systems use the same thermodynamic cycle.

7.6 Machining and 3D printing demonstrations

For machining, identify the machine, workpiece, cutting tool, relative motion, feed, cutting speed and safety guard. For 3D printing, identify the digital model, slicing, layer settings, material feed, build platform, support structures and post-processing. Record the process limitations as well as its advantages.
