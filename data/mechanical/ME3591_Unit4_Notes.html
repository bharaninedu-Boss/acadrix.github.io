1. Helical Springs

A spring is an elastic element that stores mechanical energy and releases it when the load is removed. In a close-coiled helical compression spring, the wire is primarily subjected to torsional shear stress.

Important design quantities

  • Wire diameter, d
  • Mean coil diameter, D
  • Spring index, C
  • Number of active coils, N
  • Total coils, Nt
  • Deflection, δ
  • Spring stiffness, k
C = Dd
k = Gd⁴8D³N
τ = 8FDKπd³

The Wahl factor accounts for direct shear and curvature effects in a helical spring:

K = 4C − 14C − 4 + 0.615C

For a compression spring, the design must satisfy both the allowable-stress requirement and the specified load-deflection requirement.

2. Concentric Springs

Concentric springs consist of two or more springs arranged coaxially. They are used when a higher load capacity is required within a limited space.

  • Springs may share the load.
  • Compatibility of deflection is important because the springs act together.
  • Check the stress and deflection of each spring separately.
  • Check interference and available space between the springs.

3. Surge in Springs

Surge is a spring vibration phenomenon in which the coils oscillate because the forcing frequency approaches a natural frequency of the spring. It can produce large deflections and increased dynamic stress.

Ways to control surge

  • Avoid operation near the spring's natural frequency.
  • Use suitable spring proportions.
  • Provide damping where appropriate.
  • Consider operating speed during dynamic spring design.

4. Laminated Springs

A laminated or leaf spring is formed from multiple strips of spring material. The leaves are arranged to carry load and provide controlled deflection.

Design considerations

  • Load and required deflection
  • Number and dimensions of leaves
  • Bending stress
  • Leaf contact and load distribution
  • Eye and fastening details

Use the prescribed design procedure and data-book constants for numerical proportions rather than assuming standard dimensions from memory.

5. Rubber Springs

Rubber springs store energy through elastic deformation of rubber. They can also provide damping and vibration isolation.

  • Useful for vibration isolation.
  • Properties depend strongly on rubber compound, geometry and loading.
  • Consider compression, shear and service temperature where relevant.
  • Account for long-term deformation and environmental effects in practical design.

6. Flywheels

A flywheel is a rotating energy-storage element. It absorbs excess energy when the engine torque exceeds the resisting torque and releases energy when the available torque is lower.

Key relations

Coefficient of fluctuation of speed = ωmax − ωminωmean
Kinetic energy = 12Iω²

Flywheel design therefore connects the required maximum fluctuation of energy with the allowable speed fluctuation and the moment of inertia.

Stress checks

  • Centrifugal stress in the rim
  • Bending and inertia effects in arms
  • Hub and rim connection
  • Operating speed and material strength

For punching machines, determine the energy required during the working stroke and size the flywheel so that the permitted speed reduction is not exceeded.

7. Solid and Rimmed Flywheels

A solid flywheel has its mass distributed more continuously, whereas a rimmed flywheel concentrates a large proportion of its mass near the outer radius, making it effective for storing rotational energy.

  • Rim mass strongly influences the moment of inertia.
  • Rim speed determines centrifugal stress.
  • Rimmed construction requires attention to arms and hub connections.

8. Connecting Rods

A connecting rod transmits force between the piston and crank mechanism. It experiences alternating axial loading and may experience bending and buckling effects.

Exam design sequence

  1. Determine gas and inertia forces from the supplied engine data.
  2. Find the governing axial load.
  3. Select material and allowable stress.
  4. Choose the connecting-rod section, commonly an I-section.
  5. Check compression and buckling using the prescribed column relation.
  6. Check small end and big end regions.
  7. Check cap bolts and bearing interfaces where required.
  8. Adopt practical dimensions and re-check.
σc = PA[1 + a(Lk)²]

9. Crank Shafts

A crankshaft converts reciprocating motion to rotary motion. It is subjected to combined bending and torsional loading.

Typical design sequence

  1. Determine gas force and inertia effects from the problem data.
  2. Determine connecting-rod force and resolve it into radial and tangential components.
  3. Find bearing reactions.
  4. Determine bending moments in the relevant planes.
  5. Determine torque at the critical section.
  6. Design the crankpin, main journal and shaft sections using the prescribed combined-loading method.
  7. Check crank webs, fillets and stress concentration where applicable.
  8. Adopt standard/practical dimensions and re-check.

Never design a crankshaft from torque alone when the problem supplies gas forces and bearing geometry.

1. Spring — Basic Definition

A spring is an elastic machine element that deflects under load and tends to return to its original shape when the load is removed. It is used to store energy, absorb shock/impact and control motion.

Examples visible in the notes: automobile applications, railway wagons, watches, aircraft landing gear, railway buffers and shock absorbers.

2. Functions of Springs

  • Store and release mechanical energy.
  • Cushion or reduce shock/impact loads.
  • Maintain contact between two elements.
  • Control or return motion.
  • Measure force when used in a spring balance or related instrument.

3. Types of Springs — Notebook Classification

TypeExam description
Helical springWire formed into a helix; includes compression/tension applications.
Spiral springCoiled strip used for angular energy storage/return action.
Leaf springLaminated spring used for load carrying and suspension applications.
Disc springConical/disc-shaped spring for axial loading in compact spaces.
Conical springHelical spring with varying coil diameter.

Helical spring end forms noted: open coil and closed coil. In an exam, draw the actual end condition clearly if the question asks for it.

4. Spring Stiffness

Spring stiffness is the load required per unit deflection.

k = P/δ

where P is load and δ is deflection. Units must be written consistently, such as N/mm.

5. Spring Index

Spring index is the ratio of mean coil diameter to wire diameter.

C = D/d

A practical spring design keeps the index within a suitable range so that manufacturing and stress concentration are controlled. Use the value specified by the question/data book when one is given.

6. Active Coils, Inactive Coils and Length Terms

  • Active coils: coils that participate in deflection under the applied load.
  • Inactive/dead coils: end coils that do not contribute fully to active deflection.
  • Free length: spring length without external load.
  • Solid length: length when adjacent coils are in contact; for a close-coiled spring, commonly Ls = Ntd.
  • Pitch: axial distance between corresponding points of adjacent coils in the unloaded condition.

For compression-spring free length, account for solid length, maximum working deflection and the specified clearance/allowance. Do not omit the end-condition contribution when determining total coils.

7. Core Helical-Spring Equations

C = D/d
k = Gd4/(8D3N)
δ = 8PD3N/(Gd4)
τ = Kw 8PD/(πd3)
Kw = (4C−1)/(4C−4) + 0.615/C

Here G is modulus of rigidity, N is the number of active coils and Kw is the Wahl factor used to account for curvature/direct-shear effects in the stress calculation.

8. Standard Spring-Design Workflow

  1. List load, required deflection, material/G and allowable shear stress.
  2. Choose or determine spring index C.
  3. Determine mean diameter/wire diameter relationship.
  4. Calculate Wahl factor.
  5. Determine wire diameter from the stress condition.
  6. Calculate mean coil diameter.
  7. Determine active coils from deflection/stiffness.
  8. Determine total coils from the end condition.
  9. Calculate solid length, free length and pitch.
  10. Check stress, deflection and dimensional feasibility.
  11. Consider surge where dynamic loading/speed makes it relevant.

9. Worked Classroom Example From the Supplied Notebook

The handwritten pages show a spring-design example using approximately P = 450 N, δ = 12.5 mm, D ≈ 37.5 mm and G = 0.8 × 105 N/mm², with a stated working/allowable stress of about 300 N/mm². The notebook obtains a trial wire diameter of about 5.2 mm, then C ≈ 7.2, and approximately 4 active coils; it then notes 6 total coils for the stated square-and-ground end condition.

Important verification: the handwritten stress calculation appears to use the basic torsional relation with the stress factor taken as 1 in that step, while the same notes define the Wahl factor. Therefore the 5.2 mm result should be treated as the notebook's classroom trial result, not as a universally final answer. If the exam question requires the Wahl-corrected stress, calculate Kw and re-check the selected wire diameter and coil count.

10. Second Notebook Problem — Stress in a Helical Spring

The supplied notes also show a problem with approximately Do = 80 mm, d = 12 mm, P = 1 kN, N = 105 coils and G ≈ 0.89 × 105 N/mm². The notes start by finding the mean diameter D = Do − d = 68 mm, then the spring index C = D/d and the Wahl factor.

Exam sequence: calculate D → C → Kw → maximum shear stress → deflection using the helical-spring deflection relation → factor of safety if the allowable/material strength information is supplied.

11. Common Exam Mistakes — Unit IV Springs

  • Using outside diameter where mean diameter is required.
  • Using total coils instead of active coils in the deflection equation.
  • Forgetting the Wahl factor when the problem asks for corrected maximum shear stress.
  • Confusing free length with solid length.
  • Ignoring inactive coils/end condition when finding total coils.
  • Mixing N, mm and m units.
  • Reporting a trial wire diameter without re-checking stress and deflection.
