ME3591 — Unit IV: Design of Energy Storing Elements and Engine Components

ACADRIX • Anna University R-2021 • Semester 5

Official syllabus scope: Types of springs; design of helical and concentric springs; surge in springs; laminated springs; rubber springs; flywheels considering stresses in rims and arms for engines and punching machines; solid and rimmed flywheels; connecting rods; crank shafts.

1. Helical Springs

A spring is an elastic element that stores mechanical energy and releases it when the load is removed. In a close-coiled helical compression spring, the wire is primarily subjected to torsional shear stress.

Important design quantities

  • Wire diameter, d
  • Mean coil diameter, D
  • Spring index, C
  • Number of active coils, N
  • Total coils, Nt
  • Deflection, δ
  • Spring stiffness, k
C = Dd
k = Gd⁴8D³N
τ = 8FDKπd³

The Wahl factor accounts for direct shear and curvature effects in a helical spring:

K = 4C − 14C − 4 + 0.615C

For a compression spring, the design must satisfy both the allowable-stress requirement and the specified load-deflection requirement.

2. Concentric Springs

Concentric springs consist of two or more springs arranged coaxially. They are used when a higher load capacity is required within a limited space.

  • Springs may share the load.
  • Compatibility of deflection is important because the springs act together.
  • Check the stress and deflection of each spring separately.
  • Check interference and available space between the springs.

3. Surge in Springs

Surge is a spring vibration phenomenon in which the coils oscillate because the forcing frequency approaches a natural frequency of the spring. It can produce large deflections and increased dynamic stress.

Ways to control surge

  • Avoid operation near the spring's natural frequency.
  • Use suitable spring proportions.
  • Provide damping where appropriate.
  • Consider operating speed during dynamic spring design.

4. Laminated Springs

A laminated or leaf spring is formed from multiple strips of spring material. The leaves are arranged to carry load and provide controlled deflection.

Design considerations

  • Load and required deflection
  • Number and dimensions of leaves
  • Bending stress
  • Leaf contact and load distribution
  • Eye and fastening details

Use the prescribed design procedure and data-book constants for numerical proportions rather than assuming standard dimensions from memory.

5. Rubber Springs

Rubber springs store energy through elastic deformation of rubber. They can also provide damping and vibration isolation.

  • Useful for vibration isolation.
  • Properties depend strongly on rubber compound, geometry and loading.
  • Consider compression, shear and service temperature where relevant.
  • Account for long-term deformation and environmental effects in practical design.

6. Flywheels

A flywheel is a rotating energy-storage element. It absorbs excess energy when the engine torque exceeds the resisting torque and releases energy when the available torque is lower.

Key relations

Coefficient of fluctuation of speed = ωmax − ωminωmean
Kinetic energy = 12Iω²

Flywheel design therefore connects the required maximum fluctuation of energy with the allowable speed fluctuation and the moment of inertia.

Stress checks

  • Centrifugal stress in the rim
  • Bending and inertia effects in arms
  • Hub and rim connection
  • Operating speed and material strength

For punching machines, determine the energy required during the working stroke and size the flywheel so that the permitted speed reduction is not exceeded.

7. Solid and Rimmed Flywheels

A solid flywheel has its mass distributed more continuously, whereas a rimmed flywheel concentrates a large proportion of its mass near the outer radius, making it effective for storing rotational energy.

  • Rim mass strongly influences the moment of inertia.
  • Rim speed determines centrifugal stress.
  • Rimmed construction requires attention to arms and hub connections.

8. Connecting Rods

A connecting rod transmits force between the piston and crank mechanism. It experiences alternating axial loading and may experience bending and buckling effects.

Exam design sequence

  1. Determine gas and inertia forces from the supplied engine data.
  2. Find the governing axial load.
  3. Select material and allowable stress.
  4. Choose the connecting-rod section, commonly an I-section.
  5. Check compression and buckling using the prescribed column relation.
  6. Check small end and big end regions.
  7. Check cap bolts and bearing interfaces where required.
  8. Adopt practical dimensions and re-check.

9. Crank Shafts

A crankshaft converts reciprocating motion to rotary motion. It is subjected to combined bending and torsional loading.

Typical design sequence

  1. Determine gas force and inertia effects from the problem data.
  2. Determine connecting-rod force and resolve it into radial and tangential components.
  3. Find bearing reactions.
  4. Determine bending moments in the relevant planes.
  5. Determine torque at the critical section.
  6. Design the crankpin, main journal and shaft sections using the prescribed combined-loading method.
  7. Check crank webs, fillets and stress concentration where applicable.
  8. Adopt standard/practical dimensions and re-check.

Never design a crankshaft from torque alone when the problem supplies gas forces and bearing geometry.

10. 12-Mark — Helical Compression Spring

  1. Write maximum load and required deflection.
  2. State spring material, G and allowable shear stress.
  3. Choose/identify spring index C.
  4. Calculate the Wahl factor.
  5. Determine a trial wire diameter from the stress condition.
  6. Determine mean coil diameter from C = D/d.
  7. Determine active coils from the stiffness/deflection requirement.
  8. Determine total coils from the end condition.
  9. Calculate solid length, free length and pitch.
  10. Re-check stress and actual deflection.
  11. Check surge/stability where applicable.

11. 12-Mark — Flywheel Design

  1. Draw the torque/work diagram if supplied.
  2. Determine maximum fluctuation of energy.
  3. Calculate the allowable coefficient of fluctuation of speed.
  4. Determine required moment of inertia.
  5. Choose rim dimensions and calculate rim mass.
  6. Check centrifugal stress in the rim.
  7. Design/check arms and hub.
  8. For a punching machine, include the working-stroke energy requirement.
  9. State the final mass/dimensions and safety check.

12. 12-Mark — Connecting Rod

  1. Calculate gas force.
  2. Calculate inertia force for the stated crank position.
  3. Find maximum compressive/tensile load.
  4. Select material and section.
  5. Design the shank and check buckling.
  6. Check small end and big end.
  7. Check bearing pressure and cap bolts where required.
  8. Adopt dimensions and re-check.

13. 12-Mark — Crankshaft

  1. Calculate gas force.
  2. Determine connecting-rod force.
  3. Resolve radial and tangential components.
  4. Find bearing reactions.
  5. Calculate bending moments.
  6. Calculate torque.
  7. Design critical shaft/journal sections for combined bending and torsion.
  8. Check crankpin, web, fillet and fatigue/stress concentration where required.
  9. Adopt practical dimensions and re-check.
