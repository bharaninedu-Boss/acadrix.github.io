1. Why Alternative Fuels?

Alternative fuels are fuels used instead of, or together with, conventional petrol and diesel. They are studied because automobiles require cleaner, efficient and diversified energy sources.

  • Reduce dependence on conventional petroleum fuels.
  • Use locally available energy resources.
  • Potentially reduce selected exhaust emissions.
  • Support electrification and other low-carbon vehicle technologies.

Exam point: Do not write that every alternative fuel automatically gives zero emissions. Engine type, fuel composition, calibration and operating condition affect the actual result.

2. Natural Gas in Automobiles

Natural gas is mainly methane and can be used in spark-ignition automobile engines after suitable fuel-system and control modifications. In vehicle applications it is commonly stored as compressed natural gas (CNG).

Basic CNG system

Engine modifications

  • CNG storage cylinder and high-pressure fuel lines.
  • Pressure regulator and suitable gas metering/injection system.
  • Engine control calibration for the fuel.
  • Ignition timing may need adjustment.
  • Suitable valves, seats and other components may be selected depending on engine design.

3. LPG in Automobiles

LPG is a liquefied gaseous fuel, mainly consisting of propane and butane. It is stored under pressure as a liquid and vaporized before or during metering to the engine.

Important points

  • Requires a pressurized storage tank and fuel-control system.
  • Fuel vaporization and metering must be controlled.
  • SI engines can be adapted for LPG operation.
  • Combustion and emissions depend on calibration and operating conditions.

4. Bio-diesel

Biodiesel is a diesel-engine fuel derived from biological oils or fats. It is commonly produced by converting triglyceride-based feedstock into fatty-acid esters.

Transesterification — basic idea

Vegetable oil or animal-fat feedstock reacts with an alcohol in the presence of a catalyst to produce biodiesel and glycerol.

Advantages

  • Renewable feedstock can be used.
  • Can be blended with diesel and used in suitable diesel engines.
  • Biodegradability is generally better than petroleum diesel.

Limitations

  • Fuel properties differ from conventional diesel.
  • Cold-flow, oxidation stability and storage can require attention.
  • Actual engine performance and emissions depend on blend and calibration.

5. Bio-ethanol and Gasohol

Bio-ethanol is ethanol produced from biomass. Gasohol is a gasoline–ethanol blend.

Important fuel characteristics

  • High oxygen content of ethanol can influence combustion.
  • Lower heating value than gasoline means more fuel may be needed for the same energy output.
  • Volatility, mixture preparation and cold starting need consideration.
  • Engine and fuel-system compatibility must be considered for higher ethanol blends.

6. Hydrogen in Automobiles

Hydrogen can be considered as an energy carrier/fuel for suitable vehicle power systems. It may be used in modified internal-combustion engines or in fuel-cell electric vehicles.

Important characteristics

  • Very wide flammability range.
  • High flame speed.
  • Storage is a major engineering challenge because of hydrogen's low density.
  • Combustion in an engine can still produce NOx under appropriate high-temperature conditions.

Exam focus: Explain storage methods, engine modifications and performance/emission considerations separately.

8. Performance, Combustion and Emission Characteristics

Performance

Compare brake power, torque, brake thermal efficiency, specific fuel consumption and operating range. The result depends on the fuel's heating value, mixture formation and engine calibration.

Combustion

Important factors include ignition delay, flame speed, combustion duration, knock tendency and heat-release characteristics.

Emissions

Consider CO, HC, NOx, particulate matter and CO₂ depending on the fuel and engine. Avoid absolute claims; emissions vary with engine type, operating condition and after-treatment.

9. Electric Vehicles

An electric vehicle uses electrical energy stored in a battery to drive an electric motor.

Main components

  • Battery pack
  • Battery management system
  • Power electronics / inverter
  • Electric motor
  • Controller
  • Transmission or reduction gear as required

Advantages

  • High drivetrain efficiency.
  • No tailpipe emissions during vehicle operation.
  • Regenerative braking can recover part of the kinetic energy.

Limitations

  • Charging time and charging infrastructure.
  • Battery cost, mass and lifetime.
  • Driving range depends on battery capacity and operating conditions.

10. Hybrid Electric Vehicles

A hybrid vehicle combines an internal-combustion engine with an electric propulsion system.

Types

  • Series hybrid: engine mainly drives a generator; electric motor drives the wheels.
  • Parallel hybrid: engine and electric motor can both contribute to wheel power.
  • Series-parallel: combines features of both arrangements.

Exam tip: Draw the block diagram first, then explain energy flow in each operating mode.

11. Fuel Cell Vehicles

A fuel cell converts the chemical energy of a fuel and an oxidant directly into electrical energy through an electrochemical process. In a hydrogen fuel-cell vehicle, electricity from the fuel-cell stack powers an electric motor.

Basic working

  1. Hydrogen is supplied to the anode side.
  2. Oxygen/air is supplied to the cathode side.
  3. An electrochemical reaction produces electrical energy.
  4. The electrical output drives the motor through power electronics.
  5. Water and heat are produced in a hydrogen fuel-cell system.

Advantages

  • Electric propulsion with high potential efficiency.
  • Hydrogen fuel-cell operation has water as the direct reaction product at the vehicle.
  • Fast refuelling can be possible with suitable infrastructure.

Limitations

  • Hydrogen production, storage and distribution infrastructure.
  • Fuel-cell system cost and durability considerations.
  • Overall environmental benefit depends on how hydrogen is produced.

12. Hydrogen as an Automobile Fuel — Expanded

Hydrogen is an alternative fuel/energy carrier with distinctive combustion characteristics. Your class notes emphasise its wide flammability range, low ignition energy, small quenching distance, high flame speed, high auto-ignition temperature, high diffusivity and very low density.

Properties recorded in the notes

  • Flammability range: approximately 4–75% by volume in air.
  • High flame speed.
  • Low ignition energy.
  • Small quenching distance.
  • High diffusivity.
  • Very low density.
  • Stoichiometric air–fuel ratio by mass: approximately 34:1.

Combustion significance

  • Wide flammability permits a broad mixture range.
  • High flame speed can shorten combustion duration.
  • Low ignition energy makes ignition control important.
  • High diffusivity promotes rapid mixing.
  • Low density creates a major storage/volumetric-energy challenge.

Production methods

  1. Steam reforming: hydrocarbon feedstock reacts with steam to produce a hydrogen-rich gas.
  2. Gasification: carbonaceous feedstock is converted at high temperature to producer/synthesis gas followed by suitable processing.
  3. Electrolysis: electrical energy splits water into hydrogen and oxygen.
  4. Other routes: the supplied notes mention ammonia dissociation and partial-oxidation/chemical routes.
13-mark structure: definition → properties → production → storage → engine modifications → performance → emissions → advantages/limitations.

Engine modifications

  • Suitable fuel storage and delivery system.
  • Controlled gas injection/mixing.
  • Appropriate ignition timing and control.
  • Measures against hot spots, pre-ignition and backfire.
  • ECU calibration for fuel quantity and ignition.
  • Fuel-system materials and sealing compatible with hydrogen service.

13. Hydrogen — Pre-Ignition, Backfire and NOₓ

Pre-ignition

The supplied notes associate pre-ignition with hydrogen's low ignition-energy characteristic and possible hot spots. Control requires careful ignition/mixture management and avoidance of excessive hot surfaces.

Backfire

Backfire can occur when an ignitable mixture in the intake region is ignited. The notes identify high flame speed as an important factor. Injection strategy, mixture control and ignition timing are used to reduce the tendency.

NOₓ

Hydrogen contains no carbon, but an internal-combustion engine can still form thermal NOₓ at high combustion temperatures. Therefore hydrogen IC engines are not automatically NOₓ-free.

Water addition

Your notes mention water addition as a NOₓ-control method because water absorbs heat during vaporisation and can reduce peak combustion temperature.

14. Biogas as an Automobile Fuel

Biogas is produced by anaerobic digestion of biodegradable material. Your notes mention cattle waste, poultry waste and municipal/biological waste as feedstocks.

Production sequence

  1. Organic waste enters an anaerobic digester.
  2. Microorganisms decompose complex materials such as cellulose/hemicellulose into simpler compounds.
  3. Methanogenic activity produces methane-rich gas.
  4. Residual slurry leaves through the outlet.

The supplied notes indicate operation around 40–50°C and digestion periods of roughly two weeks to two months, depending on conditions.

Composition recorded in the notes

Need for purification

  • CO₂ is non-combustible and dilutes the fuel.
  • H₂S is corrosive/toxic and can damage equipment.
  • Water vapour must also be controlled for suitable fuel conditioning.

15. Scrubbing of CO₂ and H₂S from Biogas

CO₂ removal

1. Physical absorption — water scrubbing

Pressurised water is brought into contact with raw biogas in a packed/scrubbing column. CO₂ dissolves preferentially and the treated gas leaves with reduced CO₂ content.

2. Chemical absorption

The notes mention aqueous amines such as mono-, di- and tri-ethanolamine. CO₂ reacts/absorbs in the chemical solvent and the treated methane-rich gas is recovered.

3. Chemical conversion — methanation

The notes describe catalytic conversion of CO₂ with hydrogen toward methane as a route to very high-purity gas. It is more complex/costly than simple physical scrubbing.

H₂S removal

  • Physical washing can remove part of the H₂S.
  • Chemical absorption/treatment using suitable solutions or iron-salt systems can remove H₂S.
  • The purification method is selected according to required gas quality and downstream engine/system requirements.
Diagram to practise: Raw biogas → absorber/scrubber → CO₂/H₂S removal → purified methane-rich gas → engine.

17. Vegetable Oils as Automobile Fuel

The supplied notes describe vegetable oils as renewable liquid fuels for CI-engine applications, with both fuel modification and engine modification routes.

Fuel modification

  • Transesterification: converts vegetable oil into ester-type biodiesel with more suitable fuel properties.
  • Emulsification: prepares the oil as an emulsion to modify injection/combustion behaviour.

Engine modification

  • Dual-fuel operation.
  • Low-heat-rejection engine approach, as listed in the supplied notes.

Advantages

  • Renewable source.
  • Can be produced from locally available feedstocks.
  • Can be blended with diesel where suitable.
  • Liquid-fuel handling is convenient.

Limitations

  • High viscosity affects injection/atomisation.
  • Gum/deposit formation may occur.
  • Smoke and particulate emissions may increase depending on fuel/operation.
  • Long-term residue and compatibility need attention.
