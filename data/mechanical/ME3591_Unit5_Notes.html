1. Bearing classification

Sliding-contact bearings support the shaft through sliding contact between the journal and bearing surface, normally separated by lubricant during hydrodynamic operation.

Rolling-contact bearings use balls or rollers between inner and outer raceways. Their selection is based on load, life, speed, bore and standard bearing ratings.

2. Hydrodynamic journal bearings

A hydrodynamic journal bearing carries load through pressure generated in the lubricant film by relative motion between journal and bearing. Important variables include load, speed, journal diameter, bearing length, viscosity and radial clearance.

2.1 Bearing pressure

p = W / (L × d)

2.2 Sommerfeld number

S = (μN/p) × (d/c)² × (1/60)

For the rpm form, use consistent SI units before substitution. The 1/60 factor converts rpm to revolutions per second.

2.3 Raimondi & Boyd graphs

The calculated Sommerfeld number and the bearing length-to-diameter ratio are used with standard performance data to obtain quantities such as coefficient of friction, minimum film thickness ratio and oil flow.

Exam rule: Do not guess chart values. Read the value from the permitted PSG Design Data Book or the data supplied with the question.

2.4 Friction and heat

v = πdN / 60
Pf = fWv

If the performance data gives hmin/c:

hmin = (hmin/c) × c

For heat balance, use the lubricant properties and oil-flow relation specified by the problem/data source.

3. Rolling-contact bearings

Rolling-contact bearings are selected from standard catalogues/data books. Important quantities are bore diameter, static load rating C0, dynamic load rating C, equivalent dynamic load P and required rating life.

3.1 Rating life

L10 = (C/P)3   for ball bearings
C = P × L101/3

For life specified in hours, first convert the operating life to the required number of revolutions and then use the consistent life unit.

3.2 Equivalent dynamic load

For combined radial and axial loading, use the bearing catalogue factors X, Y and e supplied by the standard bearing data.

P = XVFr + YFa

First compare Fa/Fr with the applicable e value; then select the appropriate expression.

3.3 Selection sequence

  1. Determine shaft/bore requirement.
  2. Determine radial and axial loads.
  3. Determine equivalent dynamic load.
  4. Convert required life to million revolutions.
  5. Calculate required dynamic rating.
  6. Select a standard bearing with suitable bore and rating.
  7. Check static capacity.
Standard-selection rule: Never invent a bearing number, rating or catalogue factor. Use the permitted PSG Design Data Book/standard catalogue.

4. Seals

A seal is used to control the movement of fluid or lubricant between regions and to protect a machine element from unwanted external contamination.

4.1 Oil seal — primary functions

For an oil-sealed bearing arrangement, the important functions are:

  • Prevent leakage of lubricant from the machine.
  • Prevent entry of dust, dirt and moisture into the bearing or machine.

Therefore, seal selection must consider the lubricant/fluid, shaft or housing arrangement, operating conditions and the required leakage protection.

Exam-ready 2M: State the two primary functions of an oil seal: prevent lubricant leakage and prevent entry of contaminants such as dust, dirt and moisture.

4.2 Design/selection considerations

  • What fluid must be retained or excluded?
  • Relative motion between the sealing surfaces.
  • Operating temperature and pressure.
  • Material compatibility with the fluid.
  • Shaft/housing surface and dimensional requirements.
  • Expected service life and maintenance conditions.

The syllabus says “Design of Seals”; these notes keep the treatment at the level directly supported by the available course/source material rather than inventing an unsupported seal-sizing formula.

5. Gaskets

A gasket is a sealing element placed between mating stationary surfaces to prevent leakage through the joint. In a bolted joint, tightening produces bolt tension and gasket compression.

5.1 Gasket behaviour in a bolted joint

The available Bhandari source models the connected parts/gasket as an elastic member with stiffness. The applied external load changes the bolt and gasket forces according to the stiffness relationship.

kg = EgAg / Lg

where Eg is gasket modulus, Ag is effective gasket area and Lg is the effective gasket thickness/length used by the joint model.

5.2 Gasket material and joint stiffness

The source material provides different gasket/packing cases with different stiffness constants. The correct value depends on the gasket/joint construction specified in the problem.

Joint/gasket exampleDesign significance
Soft packingMore compliant sealing layer
Soft copper gasketMetallic gasket with joint stiffness contribution
Hard copper gasketStiffer gasket behaviour
Metal-to-metal jointNo separate soft gasket layer in the idealised joint model

Gasket materials represented in the available source include cork, compressed asbestos, copper-based gasket materials, rubber, spiral-wound material, Teflon and vegetable fibre. Material selection must follow the permitted design data/source and service requirements.

5.3 Gasket design approach

  1. Identify the joint type and sealing requirement.
  2. Identify the internal fluid/pressure and service environment.
  3. Select a compatible gasket material/type from the permitted data.
  4. Determine the gasket area and thickness required by the joint model/data.
  5. Account for gasket stiffness when analysing the bolted joint.
  6. Check that the gasket remains adequately compressed and leakage is controlled under service loading.
Numerical caution: If a question supplies a gasket stiffness constant, modulus or design-data value, use that value. Do not substitute a remembered value from another gasket type.

6. Seals vs Gaskets

7. Worked Unit V numerical — hydrodynamic bearing

Given: W = 3.2 kN, N = 1490 rpm, d = 50 mm, L = 50 mm, radial clearance c = 0.05 mm, lubricant viscosity μ = 25 cP.

Step 1 — Convert viscosity

1 cP = 0.001 Pa·s

μ = 25 × 0.001 = 0.025 Pa·s

Step 2 — Bearing pressure

p = W / (L × d)

p = 3200/(50 × 50)

p = 3200/2500

p = 1.28 N/mm² = 1.28 MPa

Step 3 — Sommerfeld number

S = (μN/p) × (d/c)² × (1/60)

Use p = 1.28 × 106 Pa.

d/c = 50/0.05 = 1000

S = [0.025 × 1490/(1.28 × 106)] × 1000² × (1/60)

S ≈ 0.121

Step 4 — Standard performance data

For L/d = 1 and S ≈ 0.121, the supplied worked solution gives approximately f = 0.00644, hmin/c = 0.40 and Q ≈ 0.403 L/min.

Step 5 — Minimum oil-film thickness

hmin = 0.40 × 0.05

hmin = 0.020 mm = 20 μm

Step 6 — Friction power

v = πdN / 60

v = π × 0.05 × 1490/60 ≈ 3.90 m/s

Pf = fWv

Pf = 0.00644 × 3200 × 3.90 ≈ 80.4 W

Step 7 — Heat balance

Using the supplied worked-problem lubricant properties and oil flow gives ΔT ≈ 7.9°C.

Final values: p = 1.28 MPa; S ≈ 0.121; f ≈ 0.00644; hmin ≈ 0.020 mm; Q ≈ 0.403 L/min; ΔT ≈ 7.9°C.

10. Common mistakes

  1. Using radial clearance as diametral clearance without checking the definition.
  2. Mixing mm and m in velocity/power calculations.
  3. Using the ball-bearing life exponent for a roller bearing without checking the bearing type.
  4. Ignoring axial load when the applicable catalogue criterion requires it.
  5. Guessing Raimondi–Boyd chart values.
  6. Inventing seal dimensions or gasket properties not supplied by the problem/data book.
  7. Confusing the function of a seal with the function of a stationary gasket.
