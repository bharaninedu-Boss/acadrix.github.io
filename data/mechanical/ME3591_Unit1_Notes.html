1. Introduction to Machine Design

Machine design is the systematic process of deciding the material, shape, dimensions and construction of a machine element so that it performs its required function safely and economically.

Basic design sequence

  1. Identify the function and operating requirements.
  2. Determine loads, speed, temperature and working environment.
  3. Select a suitable material.
  4. Determine possible modes of failure.
  5. Choose an appropriate failure theory and factor of safety.
  6. Calculate dimensions from strength and/or stiffness.
  7. Check manufacturability, standard sizes, assembly and maintenance.
  8. Adopt standard dimensions and prepare the final design.

2. Factors Influencing Machine Design

  • Type and magnitude of load
  • Static, fluctuating, impact or shock loading
  • Required strength and stiffness
  • Material properties and availability
  • Wear, corrosion and temperature
  • Manufacturing process and tolerances
  • Standardisation and interchangeability
  • Safety, reliability and service life
  • Space, weight and appearance
  • Cost, maintenance and ease of assembly

3. Selection of Materials

Material selection should be based on the mechanical properties required by the component and its service conditions.

Shear strengthImportant in shafts, keys and joints
Fatigue strengthImportant under repeated/fluctuating loading
ToughnessResistance to sudden fracture and impact

4. Preferred Numbers

Preferred numbers are a standardised series of numerical values used to reduce unnecessary variety in component sizes and products. They support standardisation, interchangeability and economical manufacture.

Exam point: When a calculated dimension is obtained, select a suitable standard/preferred size rather than retaining an unnecessarily precise non-standard dimension.

5. Direct, Bending and Torsional Loading

Direct normal stress: σ = P/A
Direct shear stress: τ = P/A
Bending stress: σ = M/Z
Torsional shear stress: τ = T/Zp

For a solid circular shaft, Zp = πd³/16 and bending section modulus Z = πd³/32.

6. Modes of Failure and Factor of Safety

Common failure modes include yielding, fracture, excessive deflection, fatigue failure, buckling, wear and excessive temperature.

FOS = Failure stress / Allowable stress

The allowable stress is selected below the relevant limiting strength according to the design condition and specified factor of safety.

7. Combined Loading and Principal Stresses

Machine members may experience normal stress and shear stress simultaneously. The resulting stress state should be transformed into principal stresses before applying a suitable failure theory.

σ1,2 = (σx + σy)/2 ± √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]
τmax = (σ1 − σ2)/2

8. Eccentric Loading

An eccentric load does not pass through the centroidal axis. It produces both direct loading and a moment.

M = P × e

The resulting stress is obtained by combining the direct stress and bending stress with the correct sign at the critical location.

9. Curved Beams – Crane Hook and C-Frame

In a curved beam, the neutral axis does not generally coincide with the centroidal axis. Stress distribution is therefore different from the simple straight-beam equation σ = My/I.

Typical applications include crane hooks and C-frames. Identify the critical section and use the prescribed design relation/data book for the curved-beam stress calculation.

10. Theories of Failure

Failure theories predict yielding or failure when a component is subjected to combined stresses.

  • Maximum principal stress: compares maximum principal stress with limiting normal stress.
  • Maximum shear stress (Tresca): uses maximum shear stress as the failure criterion.
  • Maximum principal strain: considers principal strains.
  • Total strain energy: compares total elastic strain energy.
  • Distortion energy (von Mises): uses distortion energy and is widely applied to ductile materials.
Numerical rule: Use the failure theory explicitly specified in the question.

11. Design Based on Strength and Stiffness

Strength design limits stress to an allowable value. Stiffness design limits deformation, deflection or twist to an acceptable value.

A component can satisfy strength but still fail its functional requirement because of excessive deformation; both checks may therefore be required.

12. Stress Concentration

Holes, keyways, grooves, shoulders and sharp corners can produce local stress intensification.

Kt = Maximum local stress / Nominal stress

For fatigue loading, notch sensitivity is used with the theoretical concentration factor to obtain an appropriate fatigue concentration factor.

13. Fluctuating Stresses and Endurance Limit

For a varying stress, identify maximum, minimum, mean and alternating stresses.

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2
σa = (σmax − σmin)/2
R = σmin/σmax

Endurance limit/strength represents the fatigue stress level associated with the specified life criterion. Surface condition, size, reliability and other applicable factors modify fatigue strength.

14. Finite and Infinite Life Design

Infinite-life design: intended to remain below the applicable fatigue limit for the specified material and conditions.

Finite-life design: designed for a specified number of cycles using the applicable fatigue-life relation or diagram.

15. Standards in Design

Standards reduce variety and improve interchangeability, manufacture, inspection and maintenance. Use the permitted PSG Design Data Book for standard dimensions, material data, preferred sizes and relevant design charts/tables.

16. 12-Mark Answer — Theories of Failure under Combined Loading

How to write: Start with the need for a failure theory, state the stress condition, explain the principal theories, give the criterion, then conclude with material/application suitability.

  1. Introduction: Machine elements frequently experience combined normal and shear stresses, so a criterion is required to predict yielding/failure.
  2. Find principal stresses: Determine σ1 and σ2 from the combined stress state.
  3. Maximum principal stress theory: failure is predicted when the maximum principal stress reaches the allowable limiting normal stress.
  4. Maximum shear stress theory: compare the maximum shear stress with the allowable shear criterion; particularly useful for ductile-material design.
  5. Maximum principal strain theory: compares the maximum principal strain with the limiting strain.
  6. Total strain-energy theory: compares the total elastic strain energy with the limiting condition.
  7. Distortion-energy theory: uses distortion energy; commonly used for ductile materials.
  8. Design decision: apply the theory specified by the question and use the appropriate allowable stress/factor of safety.
τmax = (σ1 − σ2)/2

Conclusion: Failure theories convert a multiaxial stress state into a practical design criterion. The selected criterion must match the material and stated design requirement.

17. 12-Mark Answer — Fluctuating Stress and Fatigue Design

  1. Define fluctuating stress and identify σmax and σmin.
  2. Calculate mean stress and alternating stress.
  3. Determine the stress ratio.
  4. Identify whether finite or infinite life is required.
  5. Obtain the applicable fatigue strength/life information.
  6. Apply relevant modifying factors such as surface, size and reliability when required.
  7. Use the specified fatigue design relation/diagram and factor of safety.
  8. Check the final dimension or allowable stress against the required service life.
σm = (σmax + σmin)/2     σa = (σmax − σmin)/2

Conclusion: Fatigue design depends on both the alternating and mean stress and on the required service life.

18. 12-Mark Answer — Eccentrically Loaded Member

  1. Draw the member and locate the centroidal axis.
  2. Identify the eccentricity e.
  3. Convert the eccentric load into direct load plus an equivalent moment.
  4. Calculate direct stress from the applied load.
  5. Calculate bending stress from the eccentric moment.
  6. Combine stresses at the critical fibre using the correct sign.
  7. Determine the critical stress location.
  8. Apply the specified failure theory and factor of safety.
  9. Adopt a practical standard dimension and re-check.
M = P × e

Conclusion: Eccentric loading must never be treated as pure direct loading because the eccentricity produces an additional bending moment.

19. 12-Mark Answer — Curved Beam / Crane Hook

  1. State why curved-beam theory is required.
  2. Identify the inner and outer radii and the load location.
  3. Locate the centroidal axis and explain the neutral-axis shift.
  4. Identify the critical section, normally near the inner curvature for the relevant loading case.
  5. Use the prescribed curved-beam relation/data-book procedure.
  6. Calculate the stress distribution at the critical section.
  7. Compare the resulting stress with the allowable value using the specified factor of safety.
  8. Check the final section for strength and functional requirements.

Exam diagram: Draw a clear crane-hook/C-frame cross-section, show load, inner/outer radii, centroidal reference and critical section.

22. Numerical Problem Workflow

  1. Write Given and Find.
  2. Draw the loading diagram.
  3. Convert quantities to consistent units.
  4. Calculate direct, bending or torsional stresses.
  5. Find principal stresses if required.
  6. Apply the specified failure theory and FOS.
  7. Check strength and stiffness.
  8. Select the nearest suitable PSG standard dimension.
  9. Recalculate/check the adopted dimension.
  10. State the final answer with units.
