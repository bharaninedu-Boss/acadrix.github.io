1. Laser Scan Micrometer — Measurement Logic

Given: A laser scan micrometer is used to measure the diameter of a cylindrical component. The instrument provides two edge detections separated by a measured span.
Step 1: The laser beam is scanned across the part.
Step 2: The receiver detects the interruption/edge transitions.
Step 3: The separation between the two detected edges represents the measured diameter.
Result: The instrument provides a non-contact dimensional measurement. In an exam answer, explain laser source → scanning beam → edge detection → signal processing → displayed dimension.

2. Laser Interferometer — Displacement from Fringe Count

Practice problem: A two-beam interferometric displacement measurement records 40 complete fringe changes. For a simple double-pass arrangement, laser wavelength λ = 632.8 nm. Determine the displacement.
Step 1 — Convert wavelength
λ = 632.8 nm = 632.8 × 10−9 m.
Step 2 — Use the double-pass relation
N = 2dλ  ⇒  d = Nλ2
Step 3 — Substitute
d = 40 × 632.8 × 10−92 m
Step 4 — Arithmetic
40 × 632.8 × 10−9 = 25,312 × 10−9 m = 25.312 μm.
25.312/2 = 12.656 μm.
Final answer: displacement d = 12.656 μm for the stated double-pass arrangement.
The factor of 2 depends on the optical arrangement. Always use the relation appropriate to the interferometer configuration shown in the question.

3. Ball Bar Test — Length Change

Practice problem: A ball-bar has nominal length 300.000 mm. During a test its measured effective length is 300.018 mm. Determine the length error.
Step 1 — Error
Error = Measured length − Nominal length
Step 2 — Substitute
Error = 300.018 − 300.000 = +0.018 mm
Final answer: length error = +0.018 mm. A ball-bar test is used to assess geometric/dynamic accuracy characteristics of a machine tool; interpretation should follow the test standard and software procedure used.

4. CMM — Coordinate Distance Calculation

Practice problem: Two measured points are P₁(20, 30, 10) mm and P₂(80, 70, 40) mm. Determine the straight-line distance.
Step 1 — Coordinate differences
Δx = 80 − 20 = 60 mm
Δy = 70 − 30 = 40 mm
Δz = 40 − 10 = 30 mm
Step 2 — Distance relation
L = √(Δx² + Δy² + Δz²)
Step 3 — Substitute
L = √(60² + 40² + 30²) = √(3600 + 1600 + 900) = √6100
Step 4 — Arithmetic
√6100 ≈ 78.102 mm.
Final answer: measured point-to-point distance ≈ 78.102 mm.

5. CMM — Basic Measurement Workflow

Full-working procedure:
  1. Inspect and clean the component.
  2. Choose a suitable CMM and probe.
  3. Mount the component securely.
  4. Establish the required alignment/datum reference frame.
  5. Qualify the probe as required.
  6. Measure the required points/features.
  7. Construct geometric entities from the measured points.
  8. Calculate dimensions/deviations using the CMM software.
  9. Compare results with drawing tolerances.
  10. Record the inspection report.

6. Multi-Sensor CMM — Selection Problem

Problem type: Select a suitable sensor for a component containing both accessible dimensional features and delicate/non-contact features.

  1. Identify feature material, size, accessibility and surface condition.
  2. Use a tactile probe where direct contact and discrete point acquisition are appropriate.
  3. Use an optical/vision sensor where non-contact measurement is advantageous.
  4. Use multiple sensors when one sensor cannot reliably measure all required features.
  5. Maintain a common coordinate reference and verify sensor calibration/qualification.
  6. Compare uncertainty and throughput with the required tolerance.

Conclusion: Multi-sensor CMMs combine complementary measurement principles to inspect different feature types without relying on a single sensor.

7. Machine Vision — Measurement Workflow

Part → Illumination → Lens/Optics → Camera → Image acquisition → Processing → Feature extraction → Measurement / decision
Practice question: Explain how a machine-vision system measures the diameter of a circular feature.
  1. Provide controlled illumination to create a usable image.
  2. Capture the image with the camera.
  3. Calibrate the image scale.
  4. Pre-process the image if required.
  5. Segment the circular feature from the background.
  6. Extract edge points.
  7. Fit an appropriate geometric model to the detected points.
  8. Calculate the diameter.
  9. Compare with specification limits.
  10. Output measurement/accept-reject information.

8. Online / In-Process Monitoring

In-process measurement monitors a feature or process during production rather than waiting until all manufacturing is complete.

Manufacturing → Sensor → Measurement → Decision/control → Process adjustment → Manufacturing

Advantages: early detection of drift, reduced scrap/rework, faster feedback and support for automated quality control.

9. Computed Tomography (CT)

Exam workflow:
  1. X-rays pass through the component from multiple angular positions.
  2. Detectors measure transmitted intensity.
  3. Collected projections are reconstructed computationally.
  4. A volumetric representation is generated.
  5. Internal and external features can be inspected from the reconstructed data.
  6. Dimensional/geometric evaluation is performed according to the selected measurement procedure.
Key advantage: CT can provide access to internal geometry that may be difficult or impossible to inspect directly with conventional external measurement methods.

10. White-Light Scanners

Principle: Structured/white-light optical systems project or observe light patterns on a surface and use image information to reconstruct surface geometry.
White light / projected pattern → Object surface → Camera(s) → Image processing → 3D point cloud / surface

Typical use: rapid non-contact capture of complex surface geometry for inspection, comparison and reverse-engineering-type applications.

11. Full 12-Mark Answer — CMM

Introduction: Computer Aided Metrology uses computer-controlled measurement and analysis to obtain dimensional and geometric information from manufactured components.

Principle: A CMM determines coordinates of selected points using a probe/sensor and a controlled coordinate system.

Types: Explain the relevant CMM configurations specified in the course material and compare their working envelopes and applications.

Construction: Explain structure, guideways, coordinate axes, probe system, work table and controller/software.

Probes: Explain contact and non-contact sensing according to the application.

Software: Explain feature construction, alignment, tolerance evaluation and report generation.

Applications: dimensional inspection, geometric verification, production quality control and automated inspection.

Multi-sensor CMM: Explain why tactile and optical sensors may be combined.

Conclusion: CMM provides computer-assisted, repeatable coordinate-based inspection for modern manufacturing quality control.
